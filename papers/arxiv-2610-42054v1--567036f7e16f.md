# CRoS: Causality Reward on Scrambled CoTs Improves Accuracy and Reasoning Faithfulness

Xiao Yu    Justin Chih-Yao Chen    Zaid Khan    Hyunji Lee    Mohit
Bansal Affiliation: UNC Chapel Hill
Email: [{xiaoyuu,cychen,zaidkhan,hyunjil,mbansal}@cs.unc.edu](mailto:)

###### Abstract

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) has become the
standard method for training language models to reason. However, RLVR
typically relies solely on outcome correctness, which does not guarantee
that the CoT causally contributes to the final answer. Without this
connection, outcome rewards may fail to reinforce high-quality
reasoning. Existing causality metrics assess CoT causality by perturbing
the CoT, such as deleting reasoning steps or injecting errors, and
measuring changes in the final answer. However, these perturbations
inevitably alter information in each step. Consequently, high causality
scores may reflect sensitivity to information changes introduced by
perturbation rather than true causality. Such confounding effects
further induce undesirable reasoning behaviors when these metrics are
used as training rewards, including generating less informative CoTs and
reducing exploration and self-correction. To address this problem, we
propose CRoS (Causality Reward on Scrambled CoTs), a novel CoT causality
metric that perturbs the order of reasoning steps without altering
content, thereby preserving information within each step. On Reasoning
Gym with Gemma-3-4B, CRoS outperforms existing causality and outcome
rewards in out-of-domain accuracy, CoT quality, and CoT faithfulness.
Specifically, CRoS achieves 46.8% accuracy on five unseen logic and
games datasets, outperforming the strongest baseline (43.8%). It also
achieves better CoT quality, consistently outperforming baselines in
CoT-Pass@K from K=1 to 128, averaging 43.4% vs. 41.4%. For CoT
faithfulness, CRoS achieves a hint acknowledgment score of 34.0% vs.
20.2%. ¹¹ 1 Project page:
[xxiaoyuuuu.github.io/CRoS-Page](https://xxiaoyuuuu.github.io/CRoS-Page).

## 1 Introduction

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) has become a
standard and popular post-training method for training language models
to reason ([Guo et al., 2025](#bib.bib3); [Shao et al.,
2024](#bib.bib4); [Yang et al., 2025](#bib.bib2); [Jaech et al.,
2024](#bib.bib1)). The model generates a chain of thought (CoT) followed
by a final answer. It then receives an outcome reward, which typically
depends solely on whether the final answer is correct. However, a model
can still receive an outcome reward even when its CoT does not causally
contribute to the final answer, as illustrated in
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ CRoS: Causality Reward on Scrambled CoTs Improves Accuracy and Reasoning Faithfulness")
(Top-left). In particular, a model may produce a final answer without
relying on its CoT, and use the CoT merely to rationalize an answer it
has already determined ([Yu et al., 2026](#bib.bib14); [Chen et al.,
2025](#bib.bib10); [Saparov and He, 2022](#bib.bib9)). In such cases,
models may rely on shortcuts rather than reasoning to produce answers,
leading to poor performance on challenging problems. Moreover, the CoT
may fail to faithfully reflect the model’s decision-making process,
making it less reliable for monitoring model behavior. To study this
limitation, several metrics have been proposed to measure _CoT
causality_ ([Turpin et al., 2023](#bib.bib6); [Yu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib14); [Chen et al., 2025](#bib.bib10); [Zaman and
Srivastava, 2026](#bib.bib7); [Sivakumaran et al., 2026](#bib.bib8)).

Existing metrics perturb the CoT in different ways and measure the
resulting effects on the final answer. The intuition is that if the CoT
causally contributes to generating the final answer, perturbing it
should affect the final answer, as illustrated in the top-right of
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ CRoS: Causality Reward on Scrambled CoTs Improves Accuracy and Reasoning Faithfulness").
These metrics can be broadly categorized into two groups based on how
they perturb the CoT: truncation-based and error-insertion causality
metrics, as shown in the bottom part of
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ CRoS: Causality Reward on Scrambled CoTs Improves Accuracy and Reasoning Faithfulness").
The first group truncates the CoT by deleting a subset of reasoning
steps and measures the resulting changes in the final answer or its
probability ([Yu et al., 2026](#bib.bib14); [Lanham et al.,
2023](#bib.bib5)). The second group instead preserves all the original
reasoning steps while injecting errors to measure their effects on the
final answer ([Lanham et al., 2023](#bib.bib5)). However, these
perturbations also alter the content in each reasoning step, either by
removing surface-level cues or introducing incorrect information.
Consequently, a high causality score may reflect either the model’s
causal reliance on the reasoning steps or its sensitivity to the
information changes introduced by these perturbations. Furthermore, due
to these confounding factors, we find that using these metrics as
rewards can induce undesirable behaviors, including generating less
informative CoTs and reducing exploration and self-correction, rather
than strengthening its causality. This raises a question: How can we
perturb the CoT without altering the content in each reasoning step, so
as to measure CoT causality more accurately without introducing
confounding effects, and provide a better reward signal to improve
reasoning performance?

To address this problem, we propose CRoS (Causality Reward on Scrambled
CoTs). Our core idea is to measure CoT causality by reordering reasoning
steps while preserving their content, avoiding confounding effects from
sensitivity to information changes. As shown in the bottom-right panel
of
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ CRoS: Causality Reward on Scrambled CoTs Improves Accuracy and Reasoning Faithfulness"),
we perturb the order of reasoning steps without altering their content,
thereby preserving the information contained within each step.
Specifically, given a CoT and its final answer, we reorder the reasoning
steps to disrupt their original order while preserving the content of
each step. We then compare the probability of the original final answer
under the original and reordered CoT. A decrease in this probability
after reordering provides evidence of greater CoT causality.

Through extensive experiments on Reasoning Gym [Stojanovski et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib15), we show that CRoS outperforms baselines in
out-of-domain accuracy and achieves higher in-domain accuracy on
challenging problems. Our method also demonstrates improvements in CoT
quality and faithfulness. Specifically, we compare CRoS with existing
causality metrics, each used as an auxiliary reward alongside the
outcome reward, as well as with the standard outcome reward. We train
Gemma-3-4B-IT [Gemma Team et al. (2025)](#bib.bib22) on AIW (logic) and
Survo (games) from Reasoning Gym [Stojanovski et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib15), and evaluate out-of-domain generalization on five
unseen datasets from the same categories where reasoning helps [Yu et
al. (2026)](#bib.bib14). In particular, CRoS achieves an average
accuracy of 46.8%, outperforming the strongest causality baseline at
43.8% and the outcome reward at 41.8% under the out-of-domain setting.
For in-domain generalization, CRoS matches the strongest baselines at
the default difficulty with an accuracy of 34.5%, but outperforms them
at medium-hard and hard difficulty levels, achieving 33.5% compared to
33.0% and 31.5% compared to 31.0%, respectively. These results suggest
that CRoS’s advantages become more pronounced on harder problems, where
correct answers may require greater reliance on CoTs.

Beyond improving answer accuracy, an effective CoT causality reward also
enhances the quality and faithfulness of the reasoning process.
Following prior works [Wen et al. (2025)](#bib.bib18); [Yue et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib17), we further evaluate CoT quality using
CoT-Pass@$`K`$ with varying numbers of sampled responses. CRoS
consistently outperforms baselines and achieves higher CoT quality as
the number of samples increases, with an average CoT quality score of
43.4% compared to 41.4%. CRoS’s advantage also extends beyond sampling
efficiency, as shown by Pass@$`K`$ results. With 64 samples, the base
model outperforms outcome rewards in answer accuracy (86.9% vs. 85.4%),
while CRoS still achieves higher accuracy of 88.3%. For CoT
faithfulness [Chen et al. (2025)](#bib.bib10), CRoS achieves a hint
acknowledgment score of 34.0%, compared to 20.2% for the strongest
baseline. We further observe differences in reasoning behaviors: models
trained with CRoS acknowledge hints more explicitly, while those trained
with existing causality rewards may fail to acknowledge hints and even
produce incorrect calculations without acknowledging them. Overall, our
findings suggest that rewarding CoT causality without altering the
content of reasoning steps improves answer accuracy, CoT quality, and
faithfulness compared with outcome-only and existing causality rewards.

## 2 Related Work

#### CoT Causality Metrics for Evaluation.

Prior work has proposed various metrics to assess CoT causality,
primarily for evaluation rather than optimization. The intuition is that
if the model relies on the CoT to generate the final answer, perturbing
the CoT should affect the answer. [Lanham et al. (2023)](#bib.bib5)
propose Early Answering and Adding Mistakes. In particular, Early
Answering truncates the CoT at different points, removing subsequent
reasoning steps, and examines whether the final answer changes. Adding
Mistakes injects errors into reasoning steps and measures whether errors
change the final answer. Previous work also proposes two metrics:
Indirect Effect (IE) [Paul et al. (2024)](#bib.bib12), which replaces
the original CoT with an alternative CoT supporting a different answer,
and Filler Tokens [Lanham et al. (2023)](#bib.bib5), which replaces the
CoT with filler tokens of the same length. Both measure causality
through changes in the final answer. Another line of work evaluates CoT
causality by intervening on model parameters through unlearning, rather
than directly perturbing the CoT ([Tutek et al., 2025](#bib.bib16)).

#### CoT Causality Metrics for RLVR Rewards.

Recent work has extended CoT causality metrics from evaluation to
reinforcement learning [Yu et al. (2026)](#bib.bib14); [Sivakumaran et
al. (2026)](#bib.bib8). [Yu et al. (2026)](#bib.bib14) show that RLVR
does not guarantee causally important or sufficient reasoning,
motivating the use of causality metrics as training rewards. They
propose Causal Importance of Reasoning (CIR) and Sufficiency of
Reasoning (SR) and use them as RLVR rewards. In particular, CIR measures
CoT causality through truncation. However, rather than checking whether
the final answer changes, CIR measures changes in the probability of the
original answer, providing a more fine-grained measure of causality. Our
work similarly uses answer probabilities but adopts a different
perturbation strategy that preserves the content of each reasoning step.
SR evaluates whether the CoT contains sufficient information to derive
the final answer rather than directly measuring causality. Specifically,
it compares answers generated by an additional model given the CoT alone
or together with the question. Similarly, [Sivakumaran et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib8) propose ReMUL, which rewards CoTs based on whether
multiple listener models can derive consistent answers from them,
primarily focusing on sufficiency rather than causality.

## 3 Method

In this section, we first discuss the limitations of existing CoT
causality metrics and motivate our
approach (Section [3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Motivation ‣ 3 Method ‣ CRoS: Causality Reward on Scrambled CoTs Improves Accuracy and Reasoning Faithfulness")).
We then introduce CRoS, our proposed causality metric based on scrambled
CoTs (Section [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Causality Reward on Scrambled CoTs ‣ 3 Method ‣ CRoS: Causality Reward on Scrambled CoTs Improves Accuracy and Reasoning Faithfulness"))
and describe how we incorporate it as an auxiliary reward into RL
training (Section [3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 CRoS as an Auxiliary Reward ‣ 3 Method ‣ CRoS: Causality Reward on Scrambled CoTs Improves Accuracy and Reasoning Faithfulness")).

### 3.1 Motivation

Existing causality metrics [Lanham et al. (2023)](#bib.bib5); [Yu et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib14) measure CoT causality by perturbing the CoT, such as
deleting reasoning steps or injecting errors into them. However, these
perturbations simultaneously alter the content in the CoT, making it
difficult to distinguish high CoT causality from sensitivity to
information changes, as illustrated in the bottom of
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ CRoS: Causality Reward on Scrambled CoTs Improves Accuracy and Reasoning Faithfulness").
We propose CRoS, a novel CoT causality metric that perturbs the order of
reasoning steps without modifying their content, thereby preserving the
information contained in each step. The key intuition is that if the CoT
causally contributes to the final answer, disrupting the order of
reasoning steps while preserving the information in each step should
reduce the probability of the original answer. We further incorporate
CRoS as an auxiliary reward alongside outcome rewards to explicitly
optimize CoT causality during RL training.

### 3.2 Causality Reward on Scrambled CoTs

Given a question $`x`$, the model generates a response $`r`$ consisting
of a step-by-step CoT $`s`$ followed by a final answer $`a`$, i.e.,
$`r=(s,a)`$. We segment the CoT by line breaks, treating each non-empty
line as one reasoning step. Therefore, the step-by-step CoT consists of
a sequence of $`t`$ reasoning steps, $`s=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{t})`$. Our
goal is not to identify the exact boundaries of reasoning steps, but to
preserve semantically meaningful units rather than individual tokens.
This allows us to perturb their order while keeping the content of each
unit unchanged, minimizing information changes in each step introduced
by the perturbation. We then sample $`M`$ random permutations
$`\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{M}`$ of the $`t`$ steps. To ensure
sufficient disruption of the original ordering, we retain only
permutations with a Kendall rank correlation $`\tau`$ [Kendall
(1938)](#bib.bib26) below a threshold relative to the original step
order, with details in
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Kendall Rank Correlation ‣ CRoS: Causality Reward on Scrambled CoTs Improves Accuracy and Reasoning Faithfulness").

Using these permutations, we construct the reordered CoTs
$`\tilde{s}^{(j)}=(s_{\sigma_{j}(1)},\ldots,s_{\sigma_{j}(t)})`$, which
preserve all reasoning steps while changing their order. This
approximately preserves the CoT content while disrupting the order
between steps. We then use a frozen model to compute the likelihood of
the original final answer $`a`$ under the original and reordered CoTs.
We denote the model parameters before RL training by $`\theta_{0}`$ and
use the corresponding frozen policy $`\pi_{\theta_{0}}`$ as the
reference model to avoid introducing information from an external model.

Let $`\ell(s^{\prime})`$ denote the mean token log-probability of the
original final answer $`a`$ under the frozen policy
$`\pi_{\theta_{0}}`$, conditioned on the question $`x`$ and a CoT
$`s^{\prime}`$. The CRoS score of a response $`r=(s,a)`$ is

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
F(r)=\ell(s)-\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\ell\big(\tilde{s}^{(j)}\big),
``` |  | (1) |

A larger $`F(r)`$ means that the original answer becomes less likely
when the CoT steps are reordered, indicating that the original CoT
exhibits stronger causal influence on the final answer.

### 3.3 CRoS as an Auxiliary Reward

In the RL training, we optimize the policy $`\theta`$, initialized from
the base model parameters $`\theta_{0}`$. Given a question $`x`$, the
model generates the response $`r`$ according to the policy
$`\pi_{\theta}`$. In our setting, each question $`x`$ has a unique
ground-truth final answer, denoted by $`a^{\star}`$. Specifically, we
train the model with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) ([Shao et
al., 2024](#bib.bib4)).

#### Outcome Reward.

For each question $`x`$, GRPO samples a group of responses from
$`\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)`$ and compares their rewards within the
group to update the policy. For a response $`r=(s,a)`$, the outcome
reward depends solely on whether the final answer $`a`$ matches the
ground-truth answer $`a^{\star}`$:

|     |                                               |     |
|-----|-----------------------------------------------|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       R_{\mathrm{corr}}(r)=\mathbb{I}(a=a^{\star}).
       ```                                            |     |

#### CRoS as Reward.

The outcome reward does not explicitly incorporate the CoT itself; two
responses with the same final answer receive the same outcome reward,
regardless of how their CoTs differ. To address this limitation, we use
the CRoS score $`F(r)`$ defined in
Eq. [1](#S3.E1 "In 3.2 Causality Reward on Scrambled CoTs ‣ 3 Method ‣ CRoS: Causality Reward on Scrambled CoTs Improves Accuracy and Reasoning Faithfulness")
as an auxiliary reward, i.e., $`R_{\mathrm{CRoS}}(r)`$, to explicitly
reward stronger causal contributions of the CoT to the final answer. A
higher CRoS indicates stronger causal dependence between the CoT and the
final answer.

Since $`F(r)`$ is a log-probability difference, and its scale varies
across questions, we rank the correct rollouts within each group by
$`F(r)`$ rather than using raw scores as rewards, ensuring a consistent
reward scale for the causality term. For a question with $`G`$ sampled
responses, let $`\mathcal{C}`$ denote the correct responses in the
group. Each $`r\in\mathcal{C}`$ receives

|  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|
|  | $`\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{CRoS}}(r)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\lambda\,\rho(r),`$ |  | (2) |
|  | $`\displaystyle\rho(r)`$ | $`\displaystyle=\frac{2\,\big(\operatorname{rank}_{\mathcal{C}}(F(r))-1\big)}{|\mathcal{C}|-1}-1,`$ |  |  |

where
$`\operatorname{rank}_{\mathcal{C}}(F(r))\in\{1,\dots,|\mathcal{C}|\}`$
is the ascending rank of $`F(r)`$ among the correct responses in the
group, so $`\rho(r)\in[-1,1]`$. $`\lambda>0`$ controls the contribution
of the causality reward. We set $`R_{\mathrm{CRoS}}(r)=0`$ for incorrect
responses. When $`|\mathcal{C}|=1`$, we set $`\rho(r)=0`$.

#### Combining Rewards.

We combine the outcome reward with the CRoS and a format reward.
Specifically, for a response $`r=(s,a)`$, the combined reward is defined
as

|  |  |  |
|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
R(r)=R_{\mathrm{corr}}(r)+\beta\,R_{\mathrm{fmt}}(r)+R_{\mathrm{CRoS}}(r),
``` |  |

where $`R_{\mathrm{fmt}}(r)\in\{0,1\}`$ indicates whether the response
consists of a single non-empty CoT block followed by a single answer
block. For responses satisfying $`R_{\mathrm{corr}}(r)=1`$, the CRoS
score further distinguishes CoTs based on their causal contribution to
the final answer. Incorrect responses receive no auxiliary causality
reward. In this way, the combined reward encourages both answer
correctness and stronger CoT causality.

## 4 Experiment Setting

#### Models.

We use Gemma-3-4B-Instruct [Gemma Team et al. (2025)](#bib.bib22) as the
base model. We select this model due to computational constraints and
sufficient room for improving reasoning performance. We also evaluate
the training generalization on Gemma-3-12B-Instruct in
Appendix [C.1](#A3.SS1 "C.1 Generalization across Model Size ‣ Appendix C Training Generalization and Robustness ‣ CRoS: Causality Reward on Scrambled CoTs Improves Accuracy and Reasoning Faithfulness").

#### Baselines.

We compare against the base model, standard outcome training, and four
existing causality metrics used as auxiliary rewards alongside the
outcome reward. Specifically, we consider Early Answering [Lanham et al.
(2023)](#bib.bib5), Adding Mistakes [Lanham et al. (2023)](#bib.bib5),
and Causal Importance of Reasoning (CIR) [Yu et al. (2026)](#bib.bib14),
and Sufficiency of Reasoning (SR) [Yu et al. (2026)](#bib.bib14). Other
causality metrics, including IE and Filler Tokens, are less suitable as
training rewards under GRPO [Shao et al. (2024)](#bib.bib4); see
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Selection of Causality Metrics for RL Training ‣ CRoS: Causality Reward on Scrambled CoTs Improves Accuracy and Reasoning Faithfulness").

#### Training configuration.

All trained models receive the format reward $`\beta R_{\mathrm{fmt}}`$
with $`\beta=0.2`$: outcome training optimizes
$`R_{\mathrm{corr}}+\beta R_{\mathrm{fmt}}`$, and each causality metric,
including CRoS, is added to this reward as an auxiliary term for correct
responses only. Other details about the training configuration and
dataset are provided in
Appendix [D](#A4 "Appendix D Training Configuration and Dataset ‣ CRoS: Causality Reward on Scrambled CoTs Improves Accuracy and Reasoning Faithfulness").

#### In-Domain and Out-of-Domain Generalization Across Difficulty Levels.

We first evaluate on seven datasets from Reasoning Gym ([Stojanovski et
al., 2025](#bib.bib15)). For in-domain evaluation, we use held-out test
sets from AIW and Survo that are disjoint from the training data. For
out-of-domain evaluation, following [Yu et al. (2026)](#bib.bib14), we
select datasets where reasoning helps, with selection details provided
in
Appendix [E](#A5 "Appendix E Out-of-Domain Dataset Selection ‣ CRoS: Causality Reward on Scrambled CoTs Improves Accuracy and Reasoning Faithfulness").
Each dataset contains $`100`$ problems, totaling $`500`$ problems per
model under the default difficulty setting. We report the average
accuracy across the five datasets. For both settings, we further
evaluate generalization across difficulty levels using Reasoning Gym’s
configurable difficulty settings [Stojanovski et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib15).

#### Metric: CoT Quality Evaluation.

Based on the out-of-domain evaluation, we further investigate CoT
quality across different sampling budgets. We use CoT-Pass@$`K`$ [Wen et
al. (2025)](#bib.bib18) to measure reasoning quality. Unlike
Pass@$`K`$ [Yue et al. (2025)](#bib.bib17), which only considers
final-answer correctness, CoT-Pass@$`K`$ requires both the final answer
and the reasoning process to be correct, with reasoning correctness
assessed by DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B²² 2
[https://huggingface.co/deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B](https://huggingface.co/deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B),
following  [Wen et al. (2025)](#bib.bib18).

#### Metric: CoT Faithfulness Evaluation.

Following prior work ([Turpin et al., 2023](#bib.bib6); [Chen et al.,
2025](#bib.bib10)), we evaluate CoT faithfulness in a monitoring
scenario by examining whether the CoT explicitly acknowledges hints when
they influence the model’s final answer. Specifically, we consider four
types of hints and focus on cases where injecting a hint changes the
final answer. To maintain a comparable level of judging capability to
prior work [Chen et al. (2025)](#bib.bib10), we use
Gemini-2.5-Flash [Comanici et al. (2025)](#bib.bib25) to assess whether
the CoT acknowledges using the hint and report the fraction of such
cases. Since this metric evaluates hint acknowledgment rather than
answer correctness, the in-domain/out-of-domain distinction is less
relevant. We therefore evaluate CoT faithfulness more broadly on 27
datasets spanning 12 categories in Reasoning Gym [Stojanovski et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib15). Details are provided in
Appendix [F.2](#A6.SS2 "F.2 Details of Hint Acknowledgment Evaluation for Faithfulness ‣ Appendix F Details of CoT Faithfulness ‣ CRoS: Causality Reward on Scrambled CoTs Improves Accuracy and Reasoning Faithfulness").

### 5.1 CRoS Improves Answer Accuracy

We begin by comparing in-domain and out-of-domain accuracy of standard
outcome reward, existing causality rewards, and our proposed CRoS
reward. As shown in
Table [1](#S4.T1 "Table 1 ‣ Metric: CoT Faithfulness Evaluation. ‣ 4 Experiment Setting ‣ CRoS: Causality Reward on Scrambled CoTs Improves Accuracy and Reasoning Faithfulness"),
we report average accuracy under default difficulty and further evaluate
it across four difficulty levels in both settings.

For out-of-domain, CRoS consistently outperforms all baselines,
achieving an accuracy of 46.8% at default difficulty, compared to 43.8%
for the strongest causality baseline, SR, and 41.8% for the outcome
reward. As difficulty increases, CRoS shows a more pronounced advantage
over outcome rewards. On easy problems, CRoS achieves 56.8% accuracy
compared to 55.6% for the outcome reward, while on hard problems, the
gap widens to 22.2% versus 18.8%. A similar trend is observed in the
in-domain setting, where CRoS outperforms baselines at the two highest
difficulty levels. These results suggest CRoS is particularly effective
on challenging problems, especially compared with outcome rewards. One
possible explanation is that models may rely on shortcuts or
memorization for easier in-domain problems, whereas such strategies are
less effective on out-of-domain or challenging problems. In these
settings, models may need to rely more heavily on CoTs to derive correct
answers [Jia and Mu (2026)](#bib.bib23), making causality particularly
important.

rather than their generated CoTs, thereby reducing CoT faithfulness. In
contrast, CRoS explicitly incorporates the CoT itself into the
optimization objective, improving the CoT faithfulness score to 34.0%.

improving both faithfulness and accuracy, suggesting that the two
objectives need not be in conflict when using an appropriate causality
metric.

### 6.2 Undesirable Reasoning Behaviors

#### Truncation-based Causality Rewards.

Early Answering and CIR measure CoT causality by removing a subset of
reasoning steps and evaluating their effects on the final answer.
However, these rewards conflate the causal contribution of the reasoning
process with the information removed by truncation. As a result, models
may learn to exploit information loss rather than strengthen the CoT
causality. Therefore, models may learn to minimize the useful
information in the CoT, making the final answer more dependent on random
guessing and thus more likely to change regardless of which portion of
the CoT is truncated.

We observe two corresponding undesirable behaviors. First, models tend
to include less useful information in their CoTs. To measure this
quantitatively, we use Gemini-2.5-Flash [Comanici et al.
(2025)](#bib.bib25) to identify informative reasoning steps and evaluate
whether the CoT explicitly derives the final answer. We find that the
proportion of informative steps drops to 41% and 31% for Early Answering
and CIR, respectively, compared to 52% for outcome rewards and 54% for
CRoS, as shown in
Figure [3](#S6.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 6.1 Existing Causality Metrics Are Sensitive to Content Changes ‣ 6 Existing Causality Rewards Induce Undesirable Reasoning Behaviors ‣ CRoS: Causality Reward on Scrambled CoTs Improves Accuracy and Reasoning Faithfulness")
(a). Moreover, only 27% and 30% of their CoTs explicitly derive the
final answer, compared to 74% for CRoS, as shown in
Figure [3](#S6.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 6.1 Existing Causality Metrics Are Sensitive to Content Changes ‣ 6 Existing Causality Rewards Induce Undesirable Reasoning Behaviors ‣ CRoS: Causality Reward on Scrambled CoTs Improves Accuracy and Reasoning Faithfulness")
(b). These results suggest that truncation-based rewards encourage
models to generate less informative CoTs that often fail to explicitly
derive the final answer.

Second, models also tend to generate shorter CoTs. Specifically, Early
Answering and CIR produce only 350 and 316 tokens on average,
respectively, compared to 1,890 tokens for outcome rewards and 1,762 for
CRoS. These findings suggest that truncation-based rewards may encourage
models to exploit content loss rather than genuinely improve CoT
causality. Besides, unlike existing causality rewards, CRoS does not
suffer from CoT shortening. However, the advantages of CRoS cannot be
attributed solely to CoT length, as it achieves higher accuracy than
outcome rewards despite generating CoTs of similar length, 1,762 versus
1,890 tokens. More analysis is provided in
Appendix [G](#A7 "Appendix G CRoS’s Performance Gains Are Not Simply Due to Longer CoTs ‣ CRoS: Causality Reward on Scrambled CoTs Improves Accuracy and Reasoning Faithfulness").

#### Error-insertion Causality Rewards.

Adding Mistakes, an error-insertion causality metric, perturbs the CoT
by injecting errors into reasoning steps and rewards changes in the
final answer. To exploit the errors introduced by perturbations, models
may learn to reduce self-correction and exploration in the CoT, making
the final answer more sensitive to injected errors and thus more likely
to change, resulting in higher rewards. In particular, we measure
self-correction by detecting keywords such as “wait” or “actually”
across five out-of-domain datasets. For exploration, we measure the
fraction of steps containing computed expressions on Countdown, using
25,600 responses per model (100 problems $`\times`$ 256 samples).

We observe that models exhibit less exploration and self-correction,
both essential to reasoning. As shown in
Figure [3](#S6.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 6.1 Existing Causality Metrics Are Sensitive to Content Changes ‣ 6 Existing Causality Rewards Induce Undesirable Reasoning Behaviors ‣ CRoS: Causality Reward on Scrambled CoTs Improves Accuracy and Reasoning Faithfulness")
(d), the proportion of CoTs containing self-correction decreases from
18% for outcome to 12% for Adding Mistakes. Similarly,
Figure [3](#S6.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 6.1 Existing Causality Metrics Are Sensitive to Content Changes ‣ 6 Existing Causality Rewards Induce Undesirable Reasoning Behaviors ‣ CRoS: Causality Reward on Scrambled CoTs Improves Accuracy and Reasoning Faithfulness")
(e) shows that exploration steps decrease from 80% for outcome to 31%
for Adding Mistakes, compared to 74% for CRoS. These findings suggest
that Adding Mistakes rewards may encourage models to reduce exploration
and self-correction in reasoning rather than improve CoT causality.

## 8 Conclusion

In this paper, we propose CRoS (Causality Reward on Scrambled CoTs). We
identify a common limitation of existing causality metrics: their
perturbations also alter reasoning contents, which induce undesirable
behaviors when used as training rewards, including less informative CoTs
and reduced exploration and self-correction. To address this issue, CRoS
perturbs the order of reasoning steps while preserving their content.
Experiments show that CRoS improves answer accuracy (+3.0%),
particularly on challenging problems, as well as CoT quality (+2.0%) and
faithfulness (+13.8%). These findings highlight the importance of
preserving reasoning step content when measuring CoT causality and
demonstrate that incorporating CoT causality into RL rewards can improve
answer accuracy, CoT quality, and faithfulness.

We hope our work draws greater attention from the research community to
the potential of CoT causality as a training reward in reinforcement
learning, rather than solely as an evaluation metric. We also hope our
findings encourage a more comprehensive evaluation of CoT causality,
considering both reasoning performance and broader safety scenarios.

## Limitations and Future Work

First, we evaluate CRoS as an auxiliary causality reward within GRPO,
which we chose because it is widely used to train reasoning models.
Future work could explore its effectiveness under other training
paradigms, such as supervised fine-tuning (SFT) or distillation, where
causality metrics could guide response selection from multiple teacher
models.

Second, we hope to extend CoT causality evaluation to a broader range of
scenarios beyond answer accuracy, reasoning quality, and faithfulness.
For example, future evaluations could consider more monitoring scenarios
such as reward hacking [MacDiarmid et al. (2025)](#bib.bib28) and
sandbagging [Benton et al. (2024)](#bib.bib27). Reward hacking refers to
a model exploiting an imperfect reward function to achieve high rewards
without fulfilling the intended objective, while sandbagging refers to a
model deliberately underperforming during evaluation to conceal its true
capabilities. Furthermore, exploring how to preserve the benefits of
causality rewards while mitigating potential negative impacts on refusal
behavior and broader safety considerations remains an important
direction for future work.

## Appendix A Kendall Rank Correlation

We measure how far a scrambled CoT departs from the original order with
Kendall’s $`\tau`$ ([Kendall, 1938](#bib.bib26)). For a CoT
$`s=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{t})`$, let $`\sigma(i)`$ be the original index of
the step placed at position $`i`$. A pair of positions $`i<j`$ is
concordant if $`\sigma(i)<\sigma(j)`$ and discordant otherwise. With
$`C`$ and $`D`$ the numbers of concordant and discordant pairs,

|     |                                                 |     |     |
|-----|-------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \tau(\sigma)=\frac{C-D}{\binom{t}{2}}\in[-1,1],
       ```                                              |     | (3) |

which equals $`1`$ for the original order, $`-1`$ for the reversed
order, and $`0`$ in expectation for a uniformly random permutation.

To scramble a CoT, we draw permutations uniformly at random and accept
the first one with $`\tau(\sigma)\leq\tau_{\max}`$, up to $`200`$ draws;
if none qualifies, we keep the draw with the lowest $`\tau`$.
Permutations that leave the text unchanged are discarded. We use
$`\tau_{\max}=0.3`$, draw $`M=2`$ permutations per CoT, and exclude CoTs
with fewer than four steps.

## Appendix B Selection of Causality Metrics for RL Training

Beyond CIR and SR, we also consider incorporating existing evaluation
causality metrics into RL training. However, not all metrics are equally
suitable as training rewards under GRPO [Shao et al. (2024)](#bib.bib4),
which samples multiple rollouts for the same question and computes
advantages based on relative rewards. In particular, IE and Filler
Tokens replace the original CoT with alternative reasoning or
uninformative tokens, making the counterfactual answer independent of
the original CoT content. As a result, reward differences across
rollouts may primarily reflect differences in final answers rather than
the reasoning expressed in their CoTs, providing limited or noisy
additional training signals for optimizing CoT reasoning.

Additionally, other approaches, such as unlearning-based methods ([Tutek
et al., 2025](#bib.bib16)), require updating model parameters through
unlearning to measure CoT causality. Applying these methods as RL
rewards would require repeated unlearning updates for each sampled
rollout, making the training process time-consuming and computationally
expensive. Therefore, we exclude them from our training baselines.

## Appendix D Training Configuration and Dataset

### D.1 Training configuration

We train with GRPO [Shao et al. (2024)](#bib.bib4) for $`300`$ steps
using LoRA with rank $`32`$ and $`\alpha=32`$, applied to all linear
layers. The learning rate is fixed at $`10^{-5}`$ with no warmup. Each
training step contains $`32`$ questions, with $`8`$ rollouts sampled per
question. We use a sampling temperature of $`1.0`$, top-$`p=1.0`$, and a
maximum response length of $`4{,}096`$ tokens. For training on five
Games datasets, we use a maximum response length of $`8{,}192`$ tokens
to examine whether CRoS generalizes to longer CoTs. GRPO uses
minibatches of $`16`$ questions. For CRoS, we set the Kendall rank
correlation threshold to $`\tau=0.3`$ to ensure sufficient disruption of
the original step order and set the causality reward weight
$`\lambda=0.3`$. For other baselines, following prior work [Yu et al.
(2026)](#bib.bib14), which sets $`\lambda=1.0`$ and argues that it
provides the best trade-off, we adopt $`\lambda=1.0`$ for baselines. To
validate this choice, we additionally evaluate Adding Mistakes with
$`\lambda=0.3`$ and find that it performs worse than with
$`\lambda=1.0`$ in
Appendix [H.2](#A8.SS2 "H.2 Sensitivity to the Reward Weight ‣ Appendix H Ablation Study ‣ CRoS: Causality Reward on Scrambled CoTs Improves Accuracy and Reasoning Faithfulness").
We therefore report results using $`\lambda=1.0`$ for all baselines.

In particular, for Early Answering, we retain the first 0%, 20%, 40%,
60%, and 80% of the reasoning steps and prompt the model to generate a
final answer within 128 tokens. The causality reward is the fraction of
valid split points where the generated answer differs from the original
answer. For Adding Mistakes, we introduce incorrect conclusions into
individual reasoning steps at five split points (0%, 20%, 40%, 60%, and
80%) using a frozen Gemma-3-4B-Instruct model (temperature 0.6,
top-$`p`$ 0.9, maximum 256 tokens). The policy then continues generating
the CoT and final answer. We compute the fraction of valid interventions
where the final answer differs from the original answer. For CIR, we
truncate the CoT at ten evenly spaced points, ranging from 0% to 90% of
its total token length in increments of 10%. Then, following the
previous paper [Yu et al. (2026)](#bib.bib14), we compute the
Jensen-Shannon divergence between the probability of the original answer
at each truncation point and that given the complete CoT, averaging
across all truncation points to obtain the CIR causality score. For SR,
we remove the final answer from the CoT and use a frozen
Gemma-3-4B-Instruct model to predict the answer twice, with and without
the original question. The SR score is 1 if the two predicted answers
are identical and 0 otherwise.

### D.2 Dataset

We construct the training, validation, and test datasets using Reasoning
Gym ([Stojanovski et al., 2025](#bib.bib15)). We focus on two reasoning
categories: Logic and Games. For training, We select one training
dataset for each category: AIW for Logic and Survo for Games. AIW
consists of relational reasoning problems over siblings, friends, and
colleagues, while Survo requires filling in missing numbers in a grid to
satisfy the given row and column sums. We select these datasets because
the base model achieves relatively low accuracy on them, leaving
sufficient room for improvement through training ([Yu et al.,
2026](#bib.bib14)). Both datasets also have unique correct answers,
which is important for causality rewards based on answer changes.
Otherwise, a change in the answer may simply indicate a switch between
valid answers rather than a change in CoT causality. We further evaluate
the robustness of our method by training on either a single dataset or
multiple datasets, with details provided in
Appendix [C.2](#A3.SS2 "C.2 Training Dataset Selection and Robustness ‣ Appendix C Training Generalization and Robustness ‣ CRoS: Causality Reward on Scrambled CoTs Improves Accuracy and Reasoning Faithfulness").

The training set contains $`12{,}000`$ problems, with $`6{,}000`$
examples from AIW and Survo, respectively. We additionally construct a
disjoint validation set of $`3{,}000`$ problems, containing $`1{,}500`$
examples from each dataset.

## Appendix F Details of CoT Faithfulness

### F.1 Qualitative Examples of Faithful CoTs

We provide qualitative examples across four hint types to further
illustrate how CRoS improves CoT faithfulness. While models trained with
outcome rewards or existing causality rewards often provide seemingly
plausible reasoning to rationalize the hinted answer without
acknowledging the hint’s influence, CRoS more explicitly acknowledges
its influence in the CoT. For example, in the number sequence problem
shown in
Table [7](#A3.T7 "Table 7 ‣ C.2 Training Dataset Selection and Robustness ‣ Appendix C Training Generalization and Robustness ‣ CRoS: Causality Reward on Scrambled CoTs Improves Accuracy and Reasoning Faithfulness"),
all seven models change their correct answer from 1 to the hinted answer
0. While most baselines generate a post-hoc explanation to justify 0
without mentioning the hint, both Adding Mistakes and CRoS explicitly
acknowledge the provided hint. This suggests that preserving more of the
original information in the CoT may help improve CoT faithfulness.
Moreover, CRoS more explicitly connects the hinted answer to the
provided information, stating “Considering the given answer of 0” and
“The next number should be 0 to match the provided answer.”

We further compare Adding Mistakes and CRoS in
Table [9](#A5.T9 "Table 9 ‣ Appendix E Out-of-Domain Dataset Selection ‣ CRoS: Causality Reward on Scrambled CoTs Improves Accuracy and Reasoning Faithfulness").
In the metadata example, Adding Mistakes computes
$`15\times 0.25\times 0.0625`$, but incorrectly reports the result as
0.24609375, matching the hinted answer rather than the correct value of
0.234375. In contrast, CRoS first computes the correct answer and then
explicitly acknowledges that the metadata suggests a different answer.
Similarly, in the user-hint example, Adding Mistakes adopts the hinted
answer without acknowledging it, while CRoS explicitly refers to the
provided answer.

### F.2 Details of Hint Acknowledgment Evaluation for Faithfulness

Following prior work ([Turpin et al., 2023](#bib.bib6); [Chen et al.,
2025](#bib.bib10); [Wang et al., 2026](#bib.bib11); [Zaman and
Srivastava, 2026](#bib.bib7); [Sivakumaran et al., 2026](#bib.bib8);
[Anwar et al., 2026](#bib.bib13)), we evaluate CoT faithfulness by
injecting hints into questions and measuring whether the CoT
acknowledges the influence of the hint on the final answer. Prior
studies differ in their choice of hints, including hints that suggest
either correct or incorrect answers.

In our evaluation, we inject hints that suggest incorrect answers and
select questions that the model answers correctly without the hint. This
allows us to better attribute changes in the final answer to the
injected hint rather than to the model’s original errors. We also use
open-ended questions to reduce the possibility that the model produces
the hinted answer by random guessing. We then use Gemini-2.5-Flash as an
LLM judge to evaluate the CoT and determine whether it explicitly
acknowledges the hint’s influence. In particular, the denominator counts
examples where the model changes its originally correct answer to the
incorrect answer suggested by the hint, while the numerator further
requires the CoT to acknowledge the hint’s influence on the final
answer. A higher score indicates that the CoT more faithfully reports
the factors influencing the model’s answer.

Unlike existing causality rewards, CRoS does not induce similar
undesirable behaviors, such as shortening the CoT. To investigate
whether its performance gains can be explained by longer CoTs, we
analyze the CoT length. As shown in

by longer CoTs.

As discussed in Related Work, SR measures whether the CoT contains
sufficient information to derive the final answer. We therefore further
investigate whether combining SR with other causality rewards can
alleviate their undesirable effects on CoT length and less informative
content. For example, combining SR with CIR increases the average CoT
length from 316 to 1,376 tokens, but its accuracy remains below CRoS, at
0.382 compared to 0.468. These results further suggest that CRoS’s
advantage cannot be explained solely by CoT length, but may instead stem
from its ability to better capture CoT causality without conflating it
with the effects of information changes introduced by perturbations.
````

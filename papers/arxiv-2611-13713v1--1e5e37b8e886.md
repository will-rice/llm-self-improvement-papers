###### Abstract.

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) and autonomous agents
are creating new opportunities for hardware design optimization. This
paper examines three connected directions toward autonomous RTL and
synthesis optimization: (1) _Realistic benchmarking._ We discuss
reference-based and open-ended RTL optimization benchmarks that evaluate
optimization opportunities under realistic EDA workflows. (2)
_Tool-grounded RTL optimization._ We present Dr. RTL, a multi-agent
framework that automates critical-path analysis, RTL rewriting, and
implementation-level evaluation, while continuously improving through
reusable optimization skills. Evaluated on 20 real-world RTL designs,
Dr. RTL achieves average WNS/TNS improvements of 21%/17% with a 6% area
reduction over commercial synthesis. (3) _Design-specific synthesis
optimization._ We explore agent-driven synthesis script tuning and the
discovery of executable synthesis algorithms across intermediate
representations, technology-independent optimization, and technology
mapping. Finally, we envision AI-native design automation that jointly
explores architectural, RTL, and EDA algorithm design spaces, guided by
verification constraints and implementation feedback.

## 1. Introduction

RTL optimization and logic synthesis are two synergistic processes that
jointly determine the power, performance, and area (PPA) of digital
circuits. Given a hardware architecture, RTL optimization explores
alternative implementations through logic restructuring, resource
sharing, and sequential transformations, while synthesis and
place-and-route (P&R) tools further optimize the circuit through logic
synthesis and physical implementation ([Vahid, 2010](#bib.bib30);
[Hassoun and Sasao, 2012](#bib.bib33)). The two optimization processes
are highly synergistic: RTL structure determines the optimization
opportunities available to downstream tools, while synthesis and
physical design feedback reveals circuit bottlenecks that can guide
further RTL transformations. Achieving better PPA therefore requires
considering RTL optimization and downstream EDA optimization together
through an iterative, tool-grounded process.

This synergy also raises a fundamental question: _Which RTL optimization
opportunities remain after synthesis optimizations?_ Modern synthesis
tools can automatically simplify, restructure, and map complex logic,
potentially absorbing many source-level RTL transformations.
Consequently, an RTL rewrite that improves PPA under a weak synthesis
flow may offer little additional benefit under a stronger commercial
tool. However, existing RTL optimization studies often evaluate manually
degraded designs using open-source synthesis flows, as in
RTLRewriter ([Yao et al., 2024](#bib.bib19)) and SymRTLo ([Wang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib20)). Such evaluations may emphasize recovering injected
inefficiencies or transformations already handled by advanced synthesis.
More meaningful RTL optimization should target opportunities that remain
beyond realistic synthesis, while improvements to synthesis algorithms
themselves can further extend the achievable PPA.

Figure 1. Three connected directions toward autonomous RTL and synthesis

This perspective motivates two fundamental research challenges toward
autonomous RTL and synthesis optimization:

- •
  How to benchmark RTL optimization? Establish realistic RTL designs and
  evaluation settings that distinguish genuine optimization
  opportunities from improvements already achievable through synthesis.
- •
  How to automate and improve optimization? Develop tool-grounded agents
  that explore RTL implementations and synthesis strategies, while
  accumulating reusable optimization knowledge.

These challenges connect three research directions: realistic RTL
optimization benchmarking, agentic RTL optimization, and synthesis tool
optimization. We briefly review their recent progress and remaining
challenges below.

Benchmarking realistic RTL optimization. Recent advances in LLM-based
RTL generation ([Fang et al., 2025](#bib.bib17)) include domain-specific
models ([Liu et al., 2024a](#bib.bib8)), verification-guided
generation ([Sami et al., 2024](#bib.bib37)), and agentic
frameworks ([Zhao et al., 2025](#bib.bib38); [Deng et al.,
2025](#bib.bib39)). Corresponding benchmarks, such as VerilogEval ([Liu
et al., 2023](#bib.bib7)), RTLLM ([Lu et al., 2024](#bib.bib9)),
OpenLLM-RTL ([Liu et al., 2024b](#bib.bib42)), and CVDP ([Pinckney et
al., 2025](#bib.bib21)), primarily evaluate functional correctness. RTL
optimization additionally requires measuring implementation quality
after synthesis while preserving functional equivalence. Existing
evaluations often rely on manually degraded RTL and relatively weak
synthesis flows, leaving a gap between benchmark results and
optimization under industrial conditions. This motivates realistic
benchmarks that evaluate both known optimization targets and open-ended
improvements on original human-written RTL, with synthesis quality and
verification incorporated into the evaluation.

Tool-grounded RTL optimization. With realistic synthesis evaluation, the
next challenge is to discover RTL transformations that provide
additional PPA improvements. Their effectiveness depends on how
downstream tools restructure and map the resulting logic, making
implementation feedback essential. Existing approaches explore verified
rewriting  ([Coward et al., 2024](#bib.bib13)), LLM-based RTL
optimization ([Yao et al., 2024](#bib.bib19)), symbolic reasoning ([Wang
et al., 2025](#bib.bib20)), timing-oriented transformations ([Xu et al.,
2025b](#bib.bib22)), evolutionary optimization ([Ping et al.,
2026](#bib.bib31); [Hsin et al., 2026](#bib.bib26)), and multi-agent
collaboration ([Chang et al., 2026](#bib.bib27)). Related studies
investigate retrieval-guided circuit optimization ([Chowdhury et al.,
2024](#bib.bib35)) and PPA-aware RTL generation  ([Thorat et al.,
2025](#bib.bib23); [Tasnia et al., 2025](#bib.bib24)). Fine-grained
timing analysis ([Fang et al., 2024](#bib.bib5)) can reveal bottlenecks
that remain after synthesis, while equivalence checking ensures
correctness. These capabilities motivate self-improving agents that use
detailed EDA feedback to explore RTL transformations and learn reusable
optimization strategies.

Synthesis tool optimization. The synergy between RTL and synthesis also
suggests another optimization direction: improving the synthesis
procedures themselves. Existing synthesis techniques explore Boolean
rewriting ([Brayton et al., 2006](#bib.bib4)), technology-aware
optimization ([Chen et al., 2024](#bib.bib1)), and symbolic equality
saturation ([Yin et al., 2025](#bib.bib34)). Automated flow tuning
further explores synthesis parameters ([Ziegler et al.,
2016](#bib.bib36)), configuration selection ([Xie et al.,
2020](#bib.bib14)), and optimization sequences ([Neto et al.,
2022](#bib.bib15)). More recently, AlphaEvolve ([Novikov et al.,
2025](#bib.bib44)) has demonstrated the feasibility of AI-driven
algorithm discovery, while Self-Evolved ABC ([Yu and Ren,
2026](#bib.bib45)) applies code evolution to improve general-purpose
logic synthesis. These advances motivate design-specific synthesis
optimization, where agents can tune commercial synthesis flows and
discover customized algorithms that better exploit individual circuit
structures. Together with RTL optimization, this opens opportunities for
more comprehensive RTL–EDA co-optimization.

In this paper, we review three connected research directions toward
autonomous RTL and synthesis optimization and present our recent
progress in each direction, as illustrated
in [Figure 1](#S1.F1 "In 1. Introduction ‣ Toward Autonomous RTL and Synthesis Optimization: Benchmarking and Self-Improving Agents").
Our contributions are summarized as follows:

- •
  Realistic benchmarking of RTL optimization. We introduce two
  complementary benchmarking settings: reference-based optimization
  through RTL-OPT and open-ended optimization on original human-written
  RTL through Dr. RTL. Together, they evaluate both known optimization
  opportunities and further PPA improvements under realistic synthesis
  and formal verification workflows.
- •
  Tool-grounded, self-improving agentic RTL optimization. We present
  Dr. RTL, a multi-agent framework that combines fine-grained timing
  analysis, parallel RTL exploration, sequential equivalence checking,
  and group-relative skill learning. Across 20 human-written RTL
  designs, Dr. RTL achieves average WNS and TNS improvements of 21.3%
  and 16.9%, respectively, with a 5.8% reduction in area over commercial
  synthesis.
- •
  From synthesis flow tuning to design-specific algorithm discovery. We
  explore two levels of synthesis optimization: agent-driven script
  tuning for commercial synthesis tools and design-specific algorithm
  discovery for open-source synthesis tools. Across eight designs,
  commercial synthesis tuning improves average WNS/TNS by 7.4%/11.1%,
  while open-source algorithm discovery reduces area-delay product (ADP)
  by 18.7%.

Looking ahead, we envision a self-improving AI-driven design automation
paradigm built on verification-constrained optimization spaces. These
spaces span three levels: architecture, RTL implementation, and EDA
algorithms. At the architectural level, agents explore
microarchitectures, dataflows, and resource organizations. At the RTL
level, they optimize circuit implementations, while at the EDA tool
level, they improve synthesis and physical design procedures.
Verification defines what must be preserved at each level, establishing
the boundaries for optimization. With these spaces and constraints
clearly defined, a key research direction is to develop more effective
agentic design-space exploration across abstraction levels. Accumulated
optimization strategies and verification knowledge can support continual
self-improvement and the joint optimization of hardware implementations
and EDA tools.

## 2. Benchmarking RTL Optimization

### 2.1. Overview

RTL optimization benchmarks must evaluate both functional correctness
and implementation quality after synthesis. Since the PPA benefit of RTL
transformations depends on downstream synthesis, benchmark quality is
determined by the starting RTL designs, synthesis toolchain,
verification flow, and evaluation metrics. In particular, a realistic
benchmark should capture optimization opportunities that remain
meaningful under strong synthesis tools.

We introduce two complementary evaluation settings, as illustrated
in [Figure 2](#S2.F2 "In 2.1. Overview ‣ 2. Benchmarking RTL Optimization ‣ Toward Autonomous RTL and Synthesis Optimization: Benchmarking and Self-Improving Agents").
_Reference-based evaluation_ starts from suboptimal RTL and measures
optimization against human-optimized references. _Open-ended evaluation_
starts from original human-written RTL and measures further improvements
beyond commercial synthesis. The former provides known optimization
targets, while the latter evaluates the ability to discover additional
optimization opportunities without predefined references.

Reference-based evaluation measures improvements against human-optimized

### 2.2. Background and Related Work

RTL generation benchmarks. LLM-based hardware design has motivated
several benchmarks for evaluating RTL generation from natural-language
specifications, such as VerilogEval ([Liu et al., 2023](#bib.bib7)),
RTLLM ([Lu et al., 2024](#bib.bib9)), and OpenLLM-RTL ([Liu et al.,
2024b](#bib.bib42)). These benchmarks primarily evaluate syntactic and
functional correctness. RTL optimization additionally requires measuring
PPA improvements while preserving the original design functionality.

RTL optimization evaluations. Existing RTL optimization methods are
evaluated under different design and toolchain settings.
RTLRewriter ([Yao et al., 2024](#bib.bib19)) and SymRTLo ([Wang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib20)) explore RTL rewriting using their respective
synthesis and verification workflows. POET ([Ping et al.,
2026](#bib.bib31)) investigates power-oriented optimization, while
CODMAS ([Chang et al., 2026](#bib.bib27)) uses multi-agent collaboration
for RTL optimization. Differences in starting designs, optimization
objectives, and synthesis tools make their results difficult to compare
directly.

Many existing evaluations use manually degraded RTL, where optimization
aims to recover injected inefficiencies. Such settings provide
controlled tasks, but the measured gains depend on how degradation is
introduced and how effectively synthesis handles the resulting
structures. This motivates complementary benchmarks that evaluate both
known optimization gaps and open-ended improvement opportunities under
realistic synthesis conditions.

### 2.3. Reference-Based RTL Optimization: RTL-OPT

#### 2.3.1. Benchmark Description

RTL-OPT ([Lu et al., 2026](#bib.bib25)) introduces a reference-based
benchmark using paired suboptimal and designer-optimized RTL
implementations. Each task provides a functionally correct but
suboptimal RTL design and a corresponding human-optimized reference. The
benchmark contains 36 handcrafted optimization tasks covering arithmetic
logic, control logic, finite-state machines, and pipelined datapaths.
These tasks are designed to expose RTL-level optimization opportunities
that persist after synthesis.

#### 2.3.2. Evaluation Metrics

RTL-OPT evaluates functional correctness and post-synthesis PPA quality.
Candidate implementations are checked for functional equivalence and
synthesized to measure power, timing, and area, including total power,
worst negative slack (WNS), total negative slack (TNS), cell area, and
cell count.

Optimization capability is summarized by two fractions over all tasks.
The first is the fraction of candidates whose PPA outperforms the
suboptimal input, and the second is the fraction whose PPA outperforms
the human-optimized reference. The individual PPA metrics are still
reported alongside these fractions, so that the trade-offs among
different optimization objectives are preserved.

#### 2.3.3. Evaluation Results

Under commercial compile_ultra synthesis, 35 of the 36 human-optimized
references outperform their corresponding suboptimal implementations,
confirming that the benchmark retains meaningful optimization
opportunities after synthesis. As shown
in [Table 1](#S2.T1 "In 2.3.1. Benchmark Description ‣ 2.3. Reference-Based RTL Optimization: RTL-OPT ‣ 2. Benchmarking RTL Optimization ‣ Toward Autonomous RTL and Synthesis Optimization: Benchmarking and Self-Improving Agents"),
existing LLMs show limited ability to recover these improvements. The
highest fraction of tasks outperforming the suboptimal input is 41.7%,
while only 13.9% exceed the human-optimized reference. These results
highlight the difficulty of finding RTL transformations that translate
into better implementations under commercial synthesis.

### 2.4. Open-Ended RTL Optimization Evaluation

#### 2.4.1. Benchmark Description

Open-ended evaluation measures optimization opportunities remaining in
original human-written RTL implementations, without assuming a known
optimized reference. This setting is introduced and adopted in
Dr. RTL ([Fang et al., 2026](#bib.bib43)), using 20 designs collected
from diverse open-source projects, including processors, communication
interfaces, buffers, and signal-processing modules. The designs average
812 lines of code and three modules, with more complex structures and
hierarchies than typical small-scale RTL optimization tasks.

The evaluation uses commercial logic synthesis for implementation
quality and sequential equivalence checking (SEC) for functional
correctness. Starting from human-written RTL provides a more challenging
optimization setting, where improvements must go beyond those already
achieved by the synthesis tool.

#### 2.4.2. Evaluation Metrics

The benchmark measures PPA improvements relative to the original RTL
after commercial synthesis. Timing is evaluated using WNS and TNS, while
implementation cost is measured using cell area. SEC verifies that
candidate implementations preserve the original sequential behavior.
Power is excluded from the current optimization objective because
reliable power estimation requires workload-dependent switching
activity.

Beyond final PPA, the open-ended setting also supports evaluating
iterative optimization through functional pass rates and optimization
trajectories. These metrics characterize both the quality of the
optimized implementations and the reliability of the optimization
methods that produce them.

#### 2.4.3. Evaluation Results

As shown
in [Table 1](#S2.T1 "In 2.3.1. Benchmark Description ‣ 2.3. Reference-Based RTL Optimization: RTL-OPT ‣ 2. Benchmarking RTL Optimization ‣ Toward Autonomous RTL and Synthesis Optimization: Benchmarking and Self-Improving Agents"),
open-ended evaluation reveals substantial differences in optimization
capability under commercial synthesis. Single-shot LLMs provide limited
improvements on original human-written RTL, while representative
iterative baselines achieve average WNS improvements of 4.9%–7.1%. In
comparison, the Dr. RTL optimizer achieves average WNS and TNS
improvements of 21.3% and 16.9%, respectively, with a 5.8% reduction in
area.

These results demonstrate that meaningful RTL optimization opportunities
remain beyond commercial synthesis, while their discovery requires
effective optimization methods. The Dr. RTL framework is detailed
in [Section 3](#S3 "3. Agentic RTL Optimization with Dr. RTL ‣ Toward Autonomous RTL and Synthesis Optimization: Benchmarking and Self-Improving Agents").

## 3. Agentic RTL Optimization with Dr. RTL

### 3.1. Overview

Dr. RTL ([Fang et al., 2026](#bib.bib43)) is a tool-grounded,
self-improving agentic framework for optimizing RTL implementations
beyond commercial synthesis. As illustrated
in [Figure 4](#S2.F4 "In 2.4.1. Benchmark Description ‣ 2.4. Open-Ended RTL Optimization Evaluation ‣ 2. Benchmarking RTL Optimization ‣ Toward Autonomous RTL and Synthesis Optimization: Benchmarking and Self-Improving Agents"),
it automates the iterative optimization process through interaction with
EDA tools and continuously accumulates reusable optimization knowledge.

![Refer to caption](2611.13713v1/trajectory.png)

Figure 5. Optimization trajectories of Dr. RTL across iterations for two
designs (router and pcie). WNS, TNS, area, and optimization score
improve over successive iterations while preserving functional
equivalence. The skill-enhanced variant converges faster and achieves
better PPA than the variant without learned skills.

The framework combines two complementary components. First, a
multi-agent optimization workflow performs fine-grained critical-path
analysis, parallel RTL rewriting, and tool-based evaluation under
sequential equivalence checking (SEC). Second, group-relative skill
learning distills successful and unsuccessful optimization experiences
into reusable pattern–strategy skills. Together, these components enable
agents to explore functionally equivalent RTL implementations, learn
from implementation feedback, and improve subsequent searches without
updating the LLM parameters.

### 3.2. Background and Related Work

RTL optimization explores functionally equivalent implementations for
better post-synthesis PPA. Its effectiveness depends on downstream
synthesis, requiring tool-grounded evaluation and equivalence checking.

RTL rewriting and optimization. Existing approaches include verified
e-graph rewriting in ROVER ([Coward et al., 2024](#bib.bib13)),
LLM-guided rewriting in RTLRewriter ([Yao et al., 2024](#bib.bib19)),
symbolic reasoning in SymRTLo ([Wang et al., 2025](#bib.bib20)), and
timing-oriented transformations ([Xu et al., 2025b](#bib.bib22)).
Related studies explore retrieval-guided circuit
optimization ([Chowdhury et al., 2024](#bib.bib35)) and PPA-aware RTL
generation  ([Thorat et al., 2023](#bib.bib16); [Thorat et al.,
2025](#bib.bib23); [Tasnia et al., 2025](#bib.bib24)).

Agentic RTL optimization. Recent methods employ multi-agent
collaboration in CODMAS ([Chang et al., 2026](#bib.bib27)),
power-oriented evolutionary search in POET ([Ping et al.,
2026](#bib.bib31)), and LLM-driven evolution in EvolVE ([Hsin et al.,
2026](#bib.bib26)). Agentic RTL generation further explores multi-agent
design ([Zhao et al., 2025](#bib.bib38)), context evolution ([Deng et
al., 2026](#bib.bib40)), and reinforcement learning ([Zhang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib41)).

Tool feedback and reusable knowledge. Fine-grained RTL timing
analysis ([Fang et al., 2024](#bib.bib5)) localizes implementation
bottlenecks, while sequential equivalence checking ([Systems,
2026](#bib.bib32)) ensures correctness. Agentic memory ([Xu et al.,
2025a](#bib.bib29)) and skill learning  ([Xia et al., 2026](#bib.bib28))
enable experience reuse. Dr. RTL combines critical-path feedback,
commercial synthesis, and group-relative skill learning for
self-improving RTL optimization.

### 3.3. Method: Tool-Grounded Self-Improving RTL Optimization

Automating RTL optimization. As shown
in [Figure 3](#S2.F3 "In 2.4.1. Benchmark Description ‣ 2.4. Open-Ended RTL Optimization Evaluation ‣ 2. Benchmarking RTL Optimization ‣ Toward Autonomous RTL and Synthesis Optimization: Benchmarking and Self-Improving Agents"),
Dr. RTL formulates RTL optimization as an iterative, tool-grounded
search. Starting from an original human-written RTL implementation,
Dr. RTL searches for functionally equivalent designs with better timing
and area under a fixed commercial synthesis flow. The optimization
preserves pipeline latency and micro-architectural behavior while
allowing combinational and sequential RTL transformations.

The framework coordinates three specialized agents: (1) a timing
analysis agent that examines post-synthesis timing reports, maps
critical paths to RTL structures, and diagnoses bottlenecks; (2) an RTL
optimization agent that generates multiple candidate transformations in
parallel; and (3) an evaluation agent that runs commercial synthesis and
SEC to obtain PPA and correctness feedback. An orchestrator coordinates
these agents, records optimization trajectories, and selects the best
equivalent candidate for the next iteration.

This closed-loop workflow connects optimization decisions directly to
measured implementation quality. Fine-grained timing analysis identifies
specific bottlenecks, such as deep combinational paths, high-fanout
signals, and complex control–data interactions. Parallel exploration
evaluates different transformations under the same design constraints,
while SEC ensures that selected implementations preserve functionality.

Learning reusable optimization skills. Beyond optimizing individual
designs, Dr. RTL learns reusable knowledge from previous optimization
attempts. Each trajectory records the diagnosed bottleneck, applied RTL
transformation, and resulting PPA and SEC outcomes. Because optimization
effects depend on the design and synthesis environment, Dr. RTL
introduces _group-relative skill learning_ to identify effective
transformations under comparable conditions.

Candidates generated from the same parent RTL are compared using their
relative optimization outcomes. A skill-learning agent analyzes these
comparisons and extracts reusable _pattern–strategy_ pairs. Patterns
describe recurring bottlenecks, such as deep decode logic, high fanout,
or mux-heavy selection, while strategies describe corresponding
transformations, such as condition pre-computation, signal replication,
or arithmetic restructuring.

![Refer to caption](2611.13713v1/fig/skill.png)

Figure 6. Distilling hierarchical optimization trajectories into a
reusable skill library. Trajectories are organized across iterations,
parallel candidates, and critical paths, supporting the extraction of
optimization strategies with different confidence levels and associated
transformation templates.

The extracted skills are stored in a confidence-aware library, with
their reliability updated using empirical PPA improvements and SEC
results. Successful strategies are prioritized for reuse, while
ineffective or invalid transformations are recorded to avoid repeated
exploration. When optimizing a new design, the agent applies relevant
learned skills or explores new transformations when no suitable skill is
available. The resulting experience further updates the library,
creating a continual self-improvement loop.

### 3.4. Experimental Results

Experimental setup. We evaluate Dr. RTL on 20 original human-written
Verilog designs covering processors, communication interfaces, buffers,
signal-processing modules, and other digital systems. The designs
contain 812 lines of RTL on average. We use Synopsys Design Compiler for
commercial synthesis, the Nangate 45 nm standard-cell library ([Si2,
2018](#bib.bib10)), and Cadence JasperGold SEC ([Systems,
2026](#bib.bib32)) for verification. Each design is optimized over 10
iterations with five parallel candidates per iteration.

RTL optimization performance. As summarized
in [Table 1](#S2.T1 "In 2.3.1. Benchmark Description ‣ 2.3. Reference-Based RTL Optimization: RTL-OPT ‣ 2. Benchmarking RTL Optimization ‣ Toward Autonomous RTL and Synthesis Optimization: Benchmarking and Self-Improving Agents"),
Dr. RTL improves timing on all 20 designs, achieving average WNS and TNS
improvements of 21.3% and 16.9%, respectively, with a 5.8% reduction in
area over commercial synthesis. Nine designs achieve Pareto improvements
in timing and area, and the average SEC pass rate is 86%. These results
demonstrate substantial RTL optimization opportunities beyond commercial
synthesis, even for original human-written designs. We also visualize
the optimization trajectory
in [Figure 5](#S3.F5 "In 3.1. Overview ‣ 3. Agentic RTL Optimization with Dr. RTL ‣ Toward Autonomous RTL and Synthesis Optimization: Benchmarking and Self-Improving Agents").

Compared with RTLRewriter ([Yao et al., 2024](#bib.bib19)) and
SymRTLo ([Wang et al., 2025](#bib.bib20)) under the same commercial
evaluation flow, Dr. RTL achieves substantially larger timing
improvements. The iterative baselines use the same backbone LLM and
optimization budget, supporting the benefit of tool-grounded multi-agent
exploration and reusable optimization knowledge.

Skill generalization and interpretability. We evaluate cross-design
skill transfer through four-fold cross-validation, learning skills from
15 designs and applying them to five unseen designs in each fold.
Dr. RTL extracts 47 pattern–strategy entries: 12 high-confidence, 16
medium-confidence, six low-confidence, and 13 avoid strategies. As
illustrated
in [Figure 6](#S3.F6 "In 3.3. Method: Tool-Grounded Self-Improving RTL Optimization ‣ 3. Agentic RTL Optimization with Dr. RTL ‣ Toward Autonomous RTL and Synthesis Optimization: Benchmarking and Self-Improving Agents"),
these skills connect critical-path patterns with optimization strategies
and their empirical outcomes, providing interpretable knowledge for
future searches.

Overall, Dr. RTL demonstrates two complementary capabilities: autonomous
RTL optimization through commercial EDA feedback and continual
improvement through reusable optimization knowledge. While Dr. RTL
explores alternative implementations under a fixed synthesis procedure,
the next section extends agentic optimization to the synthesis
procedures themselves.

## 4. Agentic Synthesis Optimization and Algorithm Discovery

### 4.1. Overview

Beyond RTL optimization, further PPA improvements can be achieved by
optimizing the synthesis procedures themselves. We investigate two
complementary levels of _design-specific synthesis optimization_: (1)
agentic script tuning, which explores the configuration space of
commercial synthesis tools through tool-grounded search and reusable
optimization skills, and (2) synthesis algorithm discovery, which
develops customized optimization procedures for individual designs.
These approaches extend agentic optimization from hardware
implementations to the algorithms that produce them, enabling a broader
exploration of the RTL–synthesis optimization space.

### 4.2. Background and Related Work

Logic synthesis transforms RTL into a technology-mapped netlist through
intermediate representations, technology-independent optimization, and
technology mapping ([Brayton et al., 2006](#bib.bib4); [Hassoun and
Sasao, 2012](#bib.bib33)). Existing research improves these procedures
through technology-aware rewriting ([Chen et al., 2024](#bib.bib1)),
equality saturation ([Chen et al., 2025](#bib.bib18); [Yin et al.,
2025](#bib.bib34)), learning-guided synthesis ([Neto et al.,
2019](#bib.bib11)), and technology mapping optimization ([Neto et al.,
2021](#bib.bib12)). We focus on two complementary directions for
adapting synthesis to individual designs.

Synthesis flow tuning. Flow tuning optimizes the configurations and
sequences of existing synthesis operations. SynTunSys ([Ziegler et al.,
2016](#bib.bib36)) automates synthesis parameter exploration, while
FIST ([Xie et al., 2020](#bib.bib14)) uses feature importance to guide
parameter selection. FlowTune ([Neto et al., 2022](#bib.bib15)) explores
logic optimization flows through bandit-based search, and
FlowTuner ([Liang et al., 2021](#bib.bib6)) uses knowledge transfer for
multi-stage EDA flow tuning. These studies demonstrate the benefits of
adapting synthesis configurations to different designs. Recent advances
in AI agents create further opportunities for autonomous strategy
selection, tool interaction, and learning reusable optimization
knowledge.

Synthesis algorithm discovery. AlphaEvolve ([Novikov et al.,
2025](#bib.bib44)) demonstrates the feasibility of AI-driven algorithm
discovery through evaluator-guided code evolution. Building on this
paradigm, Self-Evolved ABC ([Yu and Ren, 2026](#bib.bib45)) applies
multi-agent code evolution to improve general-purpose logic synthesis
algorithms. However, circuit structures and optimization bottlenecks
vary across designs, and general-purpose algorithms may leave
design-specific optimization opportunities unexplored. With increasingly
capable and inexpensive AI coding, we envision customizing synthesis
algorithms for each individual design to explore its optimization space
more thoroughly and achieve better PPA.

### 4.3. Method: Agentic Script Tuning and Algorithm Discovery

illustrates two levels of agentic synthesis optimization. Script tuning
explores the configuration space of an existing synthesis engine, while

optimization procedures. Both approaches follow a feedback-driven

The framework follows an agentic workflow of _analysis, strategy

according to the characteristics and critical paths of each design.

To support self-improvement, the agent also accumulates _synthesis
optimization skills_. These skills capture effective configuration
strategies, their applicable circuit characteristics, and their observed
PPA outcomes. The accumulated knowledge guides subsequent optimization
attempts, extending Dr. RTL’s skill-based learning from RTL
transformations to synthesis strategies. This enables agents to
systematically explore the capabilities of commercial synthesis tools
while reusing experience across designs.

Design-specific synthesis algorithm discovery. Our second approach
expands the optimization space from synthesis configurations to
synthesis algorithms. In principle, a synthesis engine capable of
finding the globally optimal implementation within the allowed design
space would eliminate the need for separate RTL optimization within that
same space. In practice, existing synthesis algorithms are limited by
their circuit representations, available transformations, search
heuristics, and computational budgets. Consequently, RTL optimization
can expose better implementations that existing synthesis procedures
fail to discover directly.

This observation motivates a broader direction: _improving the synthesis
algorithms themselves_. As AI coding becomes increasingly powerful and
inexpensive, we can trade additional optimization time for better PPA by
developing a customized synthesizer for each design. Rather than
requiring a single algorithm to work well across all circuits, agents
can specialize synthesis procedures to exploit the structural
characteristics and optimization opportunities of the target design. The
objective is to explore the available implementation space more
thoroughly and approach better design-specific PPA solutions.

We explore algorithm discovery across three synthesis stages, as
illustrated
in [Figure 7](#S4.F7 "In 4.3. Method: Agentic Script Tuning and Algorithm Discovery ‣ 4. Agentic Synthesis Optimization and Algorithm Discovery ‣ Toward Autonomous RTL and Synthesis Optimization: Benchmarking and Self-Improving Agents"):

- •
  _Intermediate representation (IR)._ Agents explore circuit
  representations and decomposition procedures that expose
  design-specific structures and optimization opportunities.
- •
  _Technology-independent optimization._ Agents develop rewriting,
  factoring, restructuring, and logic-sharing algorithms tailored to the
  target circuit.
- •
  _Technology mapping._ Agents adapt functional matching, covering, and
  cell-selection algorithms to improve area and timing under the target
  technology library.

The three stages provide complementary optimization freedom, from
choosing circuit representations to transforming logic structures and
selecting physical cells. Exploring them together allows agents to
discover optimization procedures that are particularly effective for
individual designs.

Algorithm discovery follows a verification-guided code evolution loop.
The agent modifies executable synthesis code, regenerates a netlist from
the original RTL, checks functional equivalence, and evaluates area and
delay. Critical-path and implementation feedback guide subsequent
algorithm revisions, while successful program variants are retained. The
resulting customized synthesizer can reproduce the optimized
implementation from the original RTL.

### 4.4. Evaluation Results

We evaluate the two approaches on eight designs each, using commercial
and open-source synthesis environments, respectively. Script tuning
targets Synopsys Design Compiler, while algorithm discovery uses
Yosys+ABC. Both experiments use fixed RTL, design constraints, and the
Nangate 45 nm technology library.

Commercial synthesis script tuning. We evaluate script tuning on RTL
already optimized by Dr. RTL, using DC compile_ultra as the baseline.
The agent explores path grouping through group_path and retiming through
optimize_registers, combining four strategies for each in a
$`4\times 4`$ search matrix. We perform five additional search rounds
using WNS, TNS, and area feedback.

As shown
in [Table 2](#S4.T2 "In 4.3. Method: Agentic Script Tuning and Algorithm Discovery ‣ 4. Agentic Synthesis Optimization and Algorithm Discovery ‣ Toward Autonomous RTL and Synthesis Optimization: Benchmarking and Self-Improving Agents")(a),
script tuning improves WNS and TNS on all eight designs, with average
improvements of 7.4% and 11.1%, respectively, at a 1.6% average area
overhead. These results demonstrate further timing optimization
opportunities beyond Dr. RTL under commercial synthesis.

Open-source synthesis algorithm discovery. We compare design-specific
algorithm evolution with agent-tuned Yosys+ABC. Each evolved synthesis
program must generate a functionally equivalent netlist from the
original RTL.

As shown
in [Table 2](#S4.T2 "In 4.3. Method: Agentic Script Tuning and Algorithm Discovery ‣ 4. Agentic Synthesis Optimization and Algorithm Discovery ‣ Toward Autonomous RTL and Synthesis Optimization: Benchmarking and Self-Improving Agents")(b),
algorithm discovery achieves average reductions of 10.6% in area, 9.1%
in delay, and 18.7% in area–delay product (ADP), with seven of eight
designs improving both area and delay. These results demonstrate the
potential of design-specific algorithms to improve PPA beyond
agent-tuned general-purpose synthesis flows.

Together, these experiments show the benefits of agentic optimization at
both the synthesis configuration and algorithmic levels, motivating
joint exploration of RTL implementations and EDA optimization
procedures.

## 5. Future Vision: Toward Verification-Guided Agentic DSE

### 5.1. Hierarchical Optimization Spaces

Existing AI-driven hardware optimization methods largely focus on
individual design stages, such as architectural exploration, RTL
optimization, or synthesis tool optimization. However, these stages are
closely coupled, and optimizing them independently limits the available
design space. We envision a broader cross-layer optimization space
organized across three levels: architecture, RTL implementation, and EDA
algorithms, as illustrated
in [Figure 8](#S5.F8 "In 5.2. Verification-Constrained Optimization Freedom ‣ 5. Future Vision: Toward Verification-Guided Agentic DSE ‣ Toward Autonomous RTL and Synthesis Optimization: Benchmarking and Self-Improving Agents").
At the architectural level, the design space covers microarchitectures,
dataflows, and resource organizations  ([Bai et al., 2021](#bib.bib2);
[Bai et al., 2023](#bib.bib3)). At the RTL level, it includes logic
restructuring, resource sharing, and sequential transformations. At the
EDA tool level, it covers synthesis and physical design configurations,
transformations, and algorithms.

These spaces interact across abstraction levels: architectural decisions
shape the RTL implementation space, while synthesis and physical design
procedures influence the achievable PPA of different implementations.
Joint exploration can therefore discover optimization opportunities that
are difficult to identify when each stage is optimized independently.
Clearly defining these spaces and their relationships provides a
foundation for cross-layer agentic DSE.

### 5.2. Verification-Constrained Optimization Freedom

Cross-layer optimization requires a clear definition of what must be
preserved and what can be changed at each level. We envision
verification constraints that separate _verified invariants_ from
_optimization freedom_. Architectural exploration preserves functional
behavior and interface requirements, RTL optimization preserves
sequential behavior, and EDA algorithm optimization ensures netlist
correctness and implementation constraints. These requirements can be
checked through simulation, equivalence checking, or other verification
techniques.

Importantly, cross-layer exploration may involve coordinated changes
across multiple design stages. Explicit verification constraints define
which changes are valid and help maintain correctness as optimization
moves between architecture, RTL, and EDA algorithms. This enables agents
to explore a broader joint optimization space with reliable correctness
feedback.

Optimization spans architecture, RTL, and EDA algorithms, with

### 5.3. Toward Self-Improving Agentic Design-Space Exploration

Moving from single-stage optimization to cross-layer DSE introduces a
substantially larger and more interconnected search space. AI agents
offer opportunities to coordinate optimization across abstraction levels
using circuit characteristics, implementation feedback, and accumulated
experience. However, effective exploration requires deciding which
design levels to optimize, how to coordinate transformations, and how to
allocate search effort across the joint space.

This raises an important research question: _How can AI agents
continuously improve their DSE capabilities across interconnected
hardware design spaces?_ Future research can explore adaptive
cross-layer optimization strategies and the reuse of knowledge learned
from previous searches, including effective transformations, search
policies, and optimization algorithms. Ultimately, we envision
self-improving AI-native EDA systems that jointly optimize hardware
architectures, RTL implementations, and the EDA procedures used to
realize them.

## 6. Conclusion

This paper presents three connected directions toward autonomous RTL and
synthesis optimization: realistic benchmarking, agentic tool-grounded
RTL optimization, and design-specific synthesis optimization. We
introduce complementary benchmarking settings to evaluate meaningful PPA
improvements beyond synthesis, and present Dr. RTL, a self-improving
agentic framework that achieves substantial timing and area improvements
over commercial synthesis. We further demonstrate the potential of
agent-driven synthesis flow tuning and design-specific algorithm
discovery for additional PPA gains. Looking ahead, we envision
verification-guided agentic DSE across architecture, RTL, and EDA
algorithms. By organizing optimization spaces, defining verification
constraints, and accumulating reusable optimization knowledge, future
agents can progressively improve both hardware implementations and the
EDA procedures that realize them.

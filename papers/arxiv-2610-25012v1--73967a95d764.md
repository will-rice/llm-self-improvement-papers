###### Abstract

As LLM adoption surges, scaling model serving requires not only adding
more GPUs but also unlocking the substantial capacity stranded in those
already deployed. Speculative decoding brings two models into the
serving process: a small draft model proposes tokens that a larger
target model verifies. Yet compiling these models individually leaves
their shared execution outside the compiler’s optimization boundary. We
therefore ask whether co-locating the draft and target models on the
same GPU and co-compiling their execution can reclaim this stranded
capacity. We introduce Conjugate Compilation, which reframes speculative
decoding as a cross-model compilation problem and jointly optimizes the
draft and target models within their shared execution rather than
treating each in isolation. One of our key insights is that cross-model
compilation should be informed by the measured performance of the
co-located shared execution it produces: an optimization that improves a
kernel in isolation need not improve the models when they execute
together. To expand cross-model opportunities, we devise operand layouts
that interleave blocks from both models within common tiles, co-locating
GEMM and non-GEMM work while preserving their mathematics. We realize
Conjugate Compilation through a self-improving agentic search system in
which an LLM edits cross-model optimization plans using the compiler and
GPU execution as its environment. Each proposed plan is compiled and
evaluated through its co-located shared execution, whose measured score,
together with kernel-level verdicts and compiler feedback, informs
subsequent proposals, while Monte Carlo tree search accumulates rewards
to guide exploration. Across six single-GPU speculative-decoding
configurations on NVIDIA B300 and RTX 5090 GPUs, Conjugate Compilation
achieves a geometric-mean per-round speedup of $`1.37\times`$ over
conventional execution. Interleaved kernels raise this speedup to
$`1.45\times`$, reaching $`1.59\times`$ in the best case. Conjugate
Compilation also improves GPU utilization on average by $`1.46\times`$
on RTX 5090 and $`1.50\times`$ on B300. These results demonstrate the
potential of cross-model conjugate compilation guided by measurements of
the co-located shared execution.

## 1 Introduction

As demand for LLM serving grows, expanding GPU infrastructure is only
one way to increase serving capacity ([International Energy Agency,
2025](#bib.bib26)). Another is to recover the substantial capacity that
remains unused within existing deployments.
Figure [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Conjugate Compilation: Self-Improving Agentic Co-Compilation for Speculative Decoding")
shows one week of hourly GPU compute utilization measurements across
enterprise users, illustrating how low average utilization persists
despite variation across users and time. Across 6,000 measurements from
350 users collected between May and August 2026, average GPU compute
utilization is 17.6%, with 94% of users averaging below 50% ([Systalyze,
2026](#bib.bib24)). These observations motivate us to examine how
execution-informed compilation can turn more of the GPUs already
deployed into useful capacity for model serving.

Turning this capacity into useful work depends in part on compilation,
which determines how a model’s operations and data are organized for GPU
execution ([Li et al., 2020](#bib.bib32)). The common practice is to
compile and optimize each model individually ([Lu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib12)). Yet a single model’s operations may provide too
little parallel work to fill the GPU or leave compute resources
underused while waiting for data ([Ding et al., 2021](#bib.bib33); [Pope
et al., 2023](#bib.bib23)). This limitation motivates us to expand the
compiler’s optimization boundary: rather than optimize each model in
isolation, we bring multiple models into concurrent execution on the
same GPU and jointly optimize them informed by their co-located shared
execution.

![Refer to caption](2610.25012v1/figures/motivation.png)

Figure 1: Hourly GPU compute utilization across enterprise users
measured with Systalyze Utilyze from May to August 2026. The black line
shows the hourly average across users; average utilization over the full
dataset is 17.6%.

Speculative decoding provides a setting for exploring this opportunity
because two models already cooperate to generate each response. A small
draft model proposes several tokens, and a larger target model verifies
them in a single forward pass, reducing the number of target-model
passes needed for generation ([Leviathan et al., 2023](#bib.bib25)).
This cooperation, however, does not by itself coordinate how their
operations share GPU resources, nor does it bring their co-located
shared execution within the scope of compiler optimization ([Liu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib20); [Zhang et al., 2026](#bib.bib19)). We therefore pose
a central research question: without changing either model’s underlying
computations, can we coordinate and co-compile the draft and target
models to reclaim the capacity left unused when they are optimized
individually?

Speculative decoding provides a concrete setting for exploring this
opportunity because two models already cooperate to generate each
response. A small draft model proposes several tokens, and a larger
target model verifies them in a single forward pass, reducing the number
of target-model passes needed for generation ([Leviathan et al.,
2023](#bib.bib25)). Prior approaches primarily exploit this structure as
a scheduling, placement, or resource-allocation opportunity, either
distributing draft and target execution across multiple GPUs or within a
single GPU, while leaving their shared execution outside the compiler’s
optimization boundary ([Liu et al., 2025](#bib.bib20); [Zhang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib19); [Chen et al., 2026](#bib.bib17)). We instead pose a
central research question: what becomes possible when we co-locate them
on the same GPU and let their shared execution directly inform
cross-model compilation.

We introduce Conjugate Compilation, which reframes speculative decoding
as a cross-model compilation problem by bringing the draft and target
models into a common optimization boundary on the same GPU. Their
co-located shared execution uses capacity left idle by either model, but
also changes the conditions under which their operations execute. An
optimization that improves a kernel in isolation may increase
contention, disrupt beneficial overlap, or otherwise fail to improve the
models when they execute together ([Elvinger et al., 2025](#bib.bib13)).
One of our key insights is therefore that cross-model compilation should
be informed by the measured performance of the co-located shared
execution it produces. Conjugate Compilation uses this joint execution
feedback to jointly optimize the draft and target models rather than
optimizing each model independently.

Optimizing within this co-located shared execution requires a search
that can respond to how the draft and target models actually behave
together. We therefore contribute a self-improving agentic system that
couples an LLM with Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS) ([Tang et al.,
2025](#bib.bib29)), using the compiler and GPU execution as its
environment. The LLM proposes edits to cross-model compilation plans,
which the compiler checks, builds, and tunes before we measure the
performance of the resulting co-located shared execution. We use MCTS as
the agent’s search medium, organizing explored plans and propagating
their measured rewards to guide further exploration, while returning the
complete execution score, kernel-level verdicts, and compiler feedback
to the LLM to inform subsequent proposals. This coupling allows the
agent to improve its proposals from accumulated execution evidence and
directs exploration toward plans that improve the models together.

To give the agentic system choices beyond coordinating separate kernels,
we contribute an operand memory layout transformation that brings work
from both models into a common kernel. By interleaving operand blocks
within common tiles, we co-locate their work while preserving which
operands participate in each computation and where its results belong.
The interleaved kernels support GEMM and non-GEMM operation groups,
including RMSNorm ([Zhang and Sennrich, 2019](#bib.bib7)) and residual
addition ([He et al., 2016](#bib.bib40)), without changing their
mathematics. The agent explores which operation groups to interleave and
how much work to draw from each model, while the compiler optimizes
these transformations as part of the broader cross-model compilation
plan. Because an interleaved kernel that improves in isolation need not
improve the models together, Conjugate Compilation evaluates these
choices using the measured performance of the complete co-located shared
execution.

We evaluate Conjugate Compilation across six single-GPU
speculative-decoding configurations on NVIDIA RTX 5090 and B300 GPUs.
Compared with compiling and optimizing each model in isolation,
Conjugate Compilation achieves a geometric-mean per-round speedup of
$`1.37\times`$. Adding selectively interleaved kernels raises this
speedup to $`1.45\times`$, reaching $`1.59\times`$ in the best case.
Conjugate Compilation also improves GPU utilization by $`1.46\times`$ on
RTX 5090 and $`1.50\times`$ on B300, on average, respectively. Together,
these results show that cross-model conjugate compilation, informed by
the measured performance of co-located shared execution, can recover
stranded capacity by jointly optimizing multiple models on a single GPU.

### 2.1 Bringing Drafting and Verification into Shared Execution

Figure [2](#S2.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 2 Conjugate Compilation: Co-Optimizing Shared Execution ‣ Conjugate Compilation: Self-Improving Agentic Co-Compilation for Speculative Decoding")(a)
shows conventional execution of speculative decoding on a single GPU.
The green blocks denote draft-model segments and the purple blocks
denote target-model segments, with horizontal position representing
execution time. The $`\gamma`$ draft steps complete before target
verification begins, so the green and purple work does not overlap. If
one draft step takes $`T_{D}`$ and target verification takes $`T_{V}`$,
their execution takes

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       T_{\mathrm{conventional}}=\gamma T_{D}+T_{V}.
       ```                                            |     | (1) |

This ordering reflects a true dependency within each batch: target
verification cannot begin until that batch’s draft tokens are available.
The dependency, however, does not extend across batches. While the
target verifies the proposals for batch $`b`$, the draft model can
generate proposals for batch $`b+1`$.

We use a dependency-preserving multi-stream co-scheduler to expose this
concurrency on the same GPU.
Figure [2](#S2.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 2 Conjugate Compilation: Co-Optimizing Shared Execution ‣ Conjugate Compilation: Self-Improving Agentic Co-Compilation for Speculative Decoding")(b)
shows the resulting co-scheduled execution. Where purple target and
green draft blocks overlap along the time axis, segments from the two
models execute concurrently; gaps indicate intervals in which only one
stream is executing. We divide both models into ordered segments,
ranging from layers and attention/MLP halves down to individual
projection stages, and submit the target and draft segments to separate
CUDA streams. Segments within each stream execute in order, while
segments on different streams may execute concurrently unless a
cross-stream dependency orders them. Our co-scheduler inserts CUDA
events ([NVIDIA,](#bib.bib3) ) only where such coordination is required,
preserving the dependencies within each model while exposing legal
overlap.

Formally, let $`G_{\sigma}=(V,E_{\sigma})`$ denote the co-scheduled
round, where $`V`$ contains the draft and target segments and
$`E_{\sigma}`$ contains their intra-model dependencies together with any
cross-stream edges $`\sigma`$. Execution preserves every edge:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
t_{\mathrm{start}}(v)\geq t_{\mathrm{finish}}(u),\qquad\forall(u,v)\in E_{\sigma}.
``` |  | (2) |

For co-scheduling alone, $`\sigma=\emptyset`$; cross-model compilation
decisions may later introduce cross-stream edges when their placement
requires them. We capture both streams and their dependencies in a
single CUDA graph and replay the complete co-scheduled round as one
unit, removing host launch overhead while preserving the dependency
structure. This co-scheduled round realizes the co-located shared
execution that Conjugate Compilation optimizes. Its measured behavior
provides the execution feedback for the cross-model compilation
decisions introduced next.

### 2.2 Cross-Model Conjugate Compilation

Once the draft and target models are co-located, compiler decisions
interact through their shared execution. Conjugate Compilation therefore
jointly optimizes cross-model compilation plans and judges them in the
execution they change. This optimization applies even when the two
models retain separate kernels on separate CUDA streams. Their kernel
configurations can still be selected according to their effect on the
complete shared execution rather than their isolated latency. We
represent these choices through a *joint optimization plan* $`\theta`$,
which captures the cross-model compiler decisions realized within the
shared execution.

Realizing a joint optimization plan involves four components: the
*conjugated operation groups* selected for joint execution, the
*interleaving proportions* that determine how much work each operation
contributes to this joint execution, the *kernel configurations* used to
execute the resulting work, and the *scheduling arrangement* that places
it into the co-scheduled execution.

Cross-model conjugated operation groups. The agent represents the
cross-model transformations in a plan as

|     |                                             |     |     |
|-----|---------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \mathcal{G}(\theta)=\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}\},
       ```                                          |     | (3) |

where each conjugated operation group

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
g=\left(f,\{o_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K_{g}}\right),\qquad 2\leq K_{g}\leq K_{\max},
``` |  | (4) |

contains $`K_{g}`$ independent operations from the draft and target
models that share a kernel family $`f`$. These families include general
matrix multiplication (GEMM) as well as row operations such as RMSNorm,
residual addition, and softmax. The grouped operations may differ in
role and shape, while operations outside the selected groups retain
separate kernels.

Interleaving proportions. In our implementation, a selected group can be
realized as a cross-model *interleaved kernel* that executes work from
multiple operations in one kernel launch. The striped blocks in
Figure [2](#S2.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 2 Conjugate Compilation: Co-Optimizing Shared Execution ‣ Conjugate Compilation: Self-Improving Agentic Co-Compilation for Speculative Decoding")(c)
show where selected draft and target operations are replaced by such
kernels, while the remaining purple and green blocks continue to execute
as separate kernels. For GEMM, each member is decomposed into
output-tile and reduction-slice units, and the fraction of work assigned
to member $`k`$ follows its share of the group’s arithmetic work:

|     |                                                |     |     |
|-----|------------------------------------------------|-----|-----|
|     |
       ``` math
       \pi_{k}=\frac{F_{k}}{\sum_{r=1}^{K_{g}}F_{r}},
       ```                                             |     | (5) |

where $`F_{k}`$ is the arithmetic work of operation $`o_{k}`$. The
interleaving proportion is therefore derived from the selected group
rather than searched independently. A descriptor table identifies each
unit’s member, tile coordinates, reduction slice, and operand addresses,
allowing operations with unrelated shapes and separately stored operands
to execute together. Mixing their work within one launch can fill
partial waves and other execution capacity that the individual
operations would leave underutilized. Each unit nevertheless reads and
accumulates only its own operation’s data, preserving every member’s
computation. The same principle extends to non-GEMM operations such as
RMSNorm and residual addition. Interleaving is therefore one cross-model
transformation available to Conjugate Compilation rather than the
definition of the joint optimization plan itself.

Kernel configurations. For both separate and conjugated kernels,
Conjugate Compilation exposes multiple backend kernel configurations
through TVM. Rather than selecting configurations solely by isolated
kernel latency, Conjugate Compilation evaluates candidate configurations
within the co-located shared execution and uses their effect on complete
execution performance to guide selection. For interleaved GEMMs, TVM
explores the same low-level schedule space available to the
corresponding separate kernels, while row-operation kernels are selected
from the schedules exposed by their templates. Kernel configuration is
therefore part of the execution-informed optimization: even when the
draft and target remain separate kernels, their implementations can be
chosen according to how they perform together.

Scheduling arrangement. For a given co-scheduled execution, the
execution scheme in
§[2.1](#S2.SS1 "2.1 Bringing Drafting and Verification into Shared Execution ‣ 2 Conjugate Compilation: Co-Optimizing Shared Execution ‣ Conjugate Compilation: Self-Improving Agentic Co-Compilation for Speculative Decoding")
fixes the current segmentation, stream assignment, and dependencies that
determine where draft and target work may legally overlap. The
conjugated operation groups determine which target segment $`i`$ and
draft segment $`j`$ must rendezvous for joint execution. These pairings
induce a joint map $`i\mapsto j`$ that must be injective and strictly
increasing along both execution chains, preserving their relative order
and preventing cycles. A joint node $`J(i,j)`$ is the scheduling
container for this co-compiled work. Selected groups may be realized by
interleaved kernels, shown as striped blocks in
Figure [2](#S2.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 2 Conjugate Compilation: Co-Optimizing Shared Execution ‣ Conjugate Compilation: Self-Improving Agentic Co-Compilation for Speculative Decoding")(c),
while other operations retain separate kernels whose execution remains
part of the same shared optimization context. The target stream joins
the corresponding draft work before entering $`J(i,j)`$, and completion
of the joint node releases subsequent draft work through a fork.

### 2.3 Judging a Joint Optimization Plan in the Execution It Changes

Let $`\Theta_{\mathrm{legal}}`$ denote the statically legal joint
optimization plans defined in
§[2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Cross-Model Conjugate Compilation ‣ 2 Conjugate Compilation: Co-Optimizing Shared Execution ‣ Conjugate Compilation: Self-Improving Agentic Co-Compilation for Speculative Decoding").
Within this shared execution, Conjugate Compilation seeks

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\theta^{\star}\in\arg\min_{\theta\in\Theta_{\mathrm{legal}}}T_{\mathrm{round}}(\theta),
``` |  | (6) |

where $`T_{\mathrm{round}}(\theta)`$ is the measured latency of the
complete shared execution produced by joint optimization plan
$`\theta`$. After the compiler realizes a plan, we additionally verify
its numerical outputs against the reference execution before considering
its performance.

Crucially, this objective does not decompose into isolated kernel
improvements. Let $`\tau(o)`$ denote the isolated latency of operation
$`o`$, and $`\tau(g)`$ the latency of the cross-model implementation of
conjugated group $`g`$. In general,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
T_{\mathrm{round}}(\theta)\neq T_{\mathrm{round}}(\emptyset)-\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}(\theta)}\left(\sum_{o_{k}\in g}\tau(o_{k})-\tau(g)\right).
``` |  | (7) |

Shared execution may already hide part of an operation’s latency, while
a decision in the joint optimization plan can change resource
contention, overlap, and synchronization elsewhere in the round. A
faster conjugated group therefore does not necessarily produce a faster
shared execution.

We consequently judge each joint optimization plan by the complete
shared execution it produces, including the synchronization and
transformation overhead introduced by cross-model compilation. Because
the decisions in a joint optimization plan interact, their benefit must
be learned from measured shared execution rather than from isolated
kernel improvements.
§[3](#S3 "3 Self-Improving Agentic Search for Conjugate Compilation ‣ Conjugate Compilation: Self-Improving Agentic Co-Compilation for Speculative Decoding")
describes how our self-improving agentic system searches this
execution-dependent space using execution feedback.

## 3 Self-Improving Agentic Search for Conjugate Compilation

Figure 3: Conjugate compilation as a self-improving agentic loop.

The joint optimization space is combinatorial and expensive to explore:
the value of a plan is known only after compilation, and the GPU
measures the resulting shared execution. In addition, each evaluation
produces rich information—including compiler legality feedback,
kernel-level verdicts, and complete shared-execution performance—that
can guide subsequent optimization. We therefore build the self-improving
agentic loop in
Figure [3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3 Self-Improving Agentic Search for Conjugate Compilation ‣ Conjugate Compilation: Self-Improving Agentic Co-Compilation for Speculative Decoding").
MCTS selects a previously explored joint optimization plan to refine,
the LLM agent proposes structured refinements to that plan, and the
compiler and GPU evaluate the resulting candidates. The measured round
reward updates the search tree, while compiler feedback and kernel-level
verdicts inform subsequent LLM proposals.

### 3.1 MCTS-Guided Plan Selection

We organize explored joint optimization plans as a Monte Carlo tree
search (MCTS), shown on the left of
Figure [3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3 Self-Improving Agentic Search for Conjugate Compilation ‣ Conjugate Compilation: Self-Improving Agentic Co-Compilation for Speculative Decoding").
Each node represents a complete joint optimization plan, with the empty
plan $`\emptyset`$, corresponding to the co-scheduling-only baseline, as
the root, and each edge represents one refinement proposed by the LLM
agent. MCTS determines which explored plan should be refined next,
balancing further exploration of promising plans against revisiting
less-explored alternatives.

Selection follows the upper confidence bound for trees,

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\theta_{t}=\arg\max_{\theta^{\prime}\in\operatorname{ch}(\theta)}\left[\overline{Q}(\theta^{\prime})+\beta\sqrt{\frac{\ln\!\left(N(\theta)+1\right)}{N(\theta^{\prime})}}\right],
``` |  | (8) |

where $`\operatorname{ch}(\theta)`$ denotes the children of $`\theta`$,
$`N(\cdot)`$ counts visits, and $`\overline{Q}`$ is the normalized mean
backed-up reward. Progressive widening limits how quickly new
alternatives are introduced, allowing a node to gain a child only while

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
|\operatorname{ch}(\theta)|<\left\lceil\kappa\left(N(\theta)+1\right)^{\alpha}\right\rceil.
``` |  | (9) |

Once MCTS selects $`\theta_{t}`$, the LLM proposes $`m`$ candidate
refinements
(§[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 LLM-Guided Joint Optimization Proposals ‣ 3 Self-Improving Agentic Search for Conjugate Compilation ‣ Conjugate Compilation: Self-Improving Agentic Co-Compilation for Speculative Decoding")),
which become children of $`\theta_{t}`$ as progressive widening permits.
Because each node is an executable joint optimization plan, we evaluate
a child directly in the complete shared execution rather than through
simulated rollouts and propagate its measured reward through the tree.
Candidates that fail compilation or numerical validation receive the
lowest reward, while their failure information remains available to
subsequent proposals.

### 3.2 LLM-Guided Joint Optimization Proposals

Given the plan $`\theta_{t}`$ selected by MCTS, the LLM agent proposes
new joint optimization plans by refining its conjugated operation
groups. The LLM does not generate GPU kernels directly; instead, it
operates on the joint optimization plan abstraction defined in
§[2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Cross-Model Conjugate Compilation ‣ 2 Conjugate Compilation: Co-Optimizing Shared Execution ‣ Conjugate Compilation: Self-Improving Agentic Co-Compilation for Speculative Decoding").
The agent has four actions: *add* creates a new conjugated operation
group, *merge* combines existing groups, *split* separates members of a
group, and *remove* dissolves a group. Each action identifies operations
and ranges of target layers and draft steps, allowing one proposal to
apply the same transformation across multiple locations in the shared
execution.

The agent returns $`m`$ distinct proposals. Edit types, operation
identifiers, ranges, and legal placements are drawn from enumerated
choices supplied by the compiler, making each proposal either directly
interpretable or rejectable with a specific feedback code. The LLM
proposes these refinements from accumulated optimization experience
rather than from the selected plan alone. Its prompt includes the
selected plan and its recent ancestors, their measured round
performance, accumulated kernel-level verdicts, compiler feedback, legal
placements, and measurements of representative draft–target segment
pairs executed separately, concurrently, and conventionally. By
comparing a plan with its ancestors and the feedback they received, the
agent can associate changes in measured shared execution with the
decisions that produced them and use that evidence to guide subsequent
proposals. The LLM and MCTS thus serve complementary roles: the LLM
converts compiler and execution evidence into informed edits, while MCTS
determines which explored plans should be refined and which alternatives
should be revisited.

### 3.3 Learning from Compiler and GPU Feedback

The compiler and GPU form the agent’s environment. Evaluating a
candidate plan $`\theta^{\prime}_{t}`$ returns

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{E}(\theta^{\prime}_{t})=\left(r_{t},\,\mathbf{v}_{t},\,\mathbf{c}_{t}\right),
``` |  | (10) |

where $`r_{t}`$ is the measured reward, $`\mathbf{v}_{t}`$ contains
kernel verdicts, and $`\mathbf{c}_{t}`$ contains compiler feedback.

The compiler first checks the static legality constraints from
§[2.2](#S2.SS2 "2.2 Cross-Model Conjugate Compilation ‣ 2 Conjugate Compilation: Co-Optimizing Shared Execution ‣ Conjugate Compilation: Self-Improving Agentic Co-Compilation for Speculative Decoding").
A proposal is rejected before compilation if, for example, its
conjugated operations are dependent, its joint placement introduces a
cycle, a group spans more than one segment of either model, or its
required operands exceed the memory budget. Each rejection is returned
to the agent through a specific code in $`\mathbf{c}_{t}`$.

For every new conjugated group that passes these checks, the compiler
builds and tunes its cross-model kernel and applies a kernel-gain gate
before inserting it into the round. The candidate kernel and the
separately compiled kernels of its members are replayed as captured CUDA
graphs in alternating timing blocks, with weights rotated beyond the L2
cache between measurements. A group $`g`$ is admitted only if

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
v(g)=\frac{\sum_{k}\tau(o_{k})}{\widehat{\tau}(g)}>1+\max\!\left(\delta,z\widehat{s}\right),
``` |  | (11) |

where $`\widehat{\tau}(g)`$ includes all work launched by the group,
$`\delta`$ is a minimum gain margin, and $`\widehat{s}`$ is the standard
error of the paired timing relative to $`\widehat{\tau}(g)`$. Every
result, whether the group passes or fails, enters $`\mathbf{v}_{t}`$ so
the agent can learn which conjugations produce useful local
improvements.

Passing the gate establishes only that a conjugated group is locally
promising. The resulting joint optimization plan is therefore compiled
into the complete co-scheduled round and checked against the reference
execution for every draft step. We require relative agreement within
$`10^{-2}`$ and permit an argmax difference only when the reference
logits themselves form a near tie; failing positions or joint sets are
quarantined and reported to the agent.

We then measure each candidate joint optimization plan on the complete
co-scheduled round, using co-scheduling without co-compilation as the
anchor. Each timing block replays the anchor, candidate, placement
control, candidate, and anchor in that order. The placement control
preserves the candidate’s join and fork dependencies but executes the
original separately compiled kernels. Comparing the anchor with this
control measures the effect of the scheduling arrangement; comparing the
control with the candidate measures the additional effect of the
compiler transformations.

We compute the candidate’s full-round speedup over the anchor as the
median of the paired anchor-to-candidate latency ratios. To express the
search reward relative to baseline, we multiply this speedup by the
anchor’s speedup over the faster of two conventional baselines: the
compiled models executed back-to-back, or sequentially within one
captured CUDA graph. This scaling factor is fixed within a
configuration, so it does not change how MCTS ranks candidate plans.

The measured full-round reward updates the MCTS search tree, while
kernel-level verdicts and compiler feedback enter the LLM’s accumulated
optimization experience to guide subsequent proposals. Tuned kernels,
kernel verdicts, compiled joint nodes, rejection outcomes, and search
checkpoints persist across escalation phases and resumed runs. The
system therefore improves through accumulated compiler and GPU
experience, without modifying the weights of the LLM agent.
Appendix [A](#A1 "Appendix A Additional Search Details ‣ Conjugate Compilation: Self-Improving Agentic Co-Compilation for Speculative Decoding")
details search-space expansion and final plan acceptance.

## 4 Evaluation

### 4.1 Methodology

Benchmarks. We evaluate four target-draft pairs on NVIDIA B300 GPU:
Llama-3.1-8B/Llama-3.2-1B, Qwen3-14B/Qwen3-0.6B,
Qwen3-32B/Qwen3-Instruct-2507-4B, and Llama-3.1-70B/Llama-3.1-8B ([Meta,
2024a](#bib.bib38); [Meta, 2024b](#bib.bib6); [Qwen Team,
2025](#bib.bib37)). We evaluate Llama-3.2-3B/Llama-3.2-1B and
Qwen3-Instruct-2507-4B/Qwen3-0.6B on NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPU. All
experiments use batch size 8 ([Qian et al., 2024](#bib.bib5)),
speculation length $`\gamma\leq 6`$ ([Leviathan et al.,
2023](#bib.bib25); [Qian et al., 2024](#bib.bib5); [Liu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib20)), a 1024-token input sequence length ([Patel et al.,
2024](#bib.bib27)), and FP16 precision ([Qian et al., 2024](#bib.bib5)).

Evaluated systems. Conventional represents the current practice for
deploying speculative-decoding models, where the draft and target models
are compiled independently and executed sequentially ([Leviathan et al.,
2023](#bib.bib25); [Kwon et al., 2023](#bib.bib28)). Conjugate
Compilation co-locates the draft and target models on the same GPU and
jointly optimizes cross-model compilation plans using measurements of
their shared execution through the closed-loop search of
§[3](#S3 "3 Self-Improving Agentic Search for Conjugate Compilation ‣ Conjugate Compilation: Self-Improving Agentic Co-Compilation for Speculative Decoding").
A dependency-preserving multi-stream schedule realizes legal concurrent
execution, including between non-matching operators such as Tensor Core
GEMMs and CUDA Core operations. Conjugate Compilation with Interleaved
Kernels additionally replaces selected compatible operation groups with
kernels that interleave their work within one launch.

Implementation. We implement Conjugate Compilation in Python using
Apache TVM v0.25 ([Chen et al., 2018](#bib.bib36)) to import
PyTorch ([Paszke et al., 2019](#bib.bib41)) models, lower their
computation, and generate CUDA code. Our search forms a closed loop
between the agent, compiler, and GPU, where agent proposes cross-model
optimization plans, compiler turns them into joint nodes and interleaved
kernels that it tunes and builds on the TVM backend, and measurements
and compiler feedback inform subsequent proposals. We organize the
evaluated plans in a Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS) ([Browne et al.,
2012](#bib.bib39)), which propagates their measured rewards through the
tree to prioritize subsequent exploration. We use OpenAI’s
gpt-5.6-luna ([OpenAI, 2026](#bib.bib4)) as the agent.

Measurements. We run all experiments with CUDA 12.8 on NVIDIA B300 SXM6
and RTX 5090 GPUs. We run experiments 50 times, report the median, and
normalize all results to Conventional. For GPU utilization we use Nsight
Compute ([NVIDIA,](#bib.bib1) ) and collect kernel traces with
CUPTI ([NVIDIA,](#bib.bib2) ).

## 5 Related Work

Parallel speculative decoding systems coordinate drafting and
verification through runtime scheduling and placement ([Liu et al.,
2025](#bib.bib20); [Chen et al., 2026](#bib.bib17)). GPU-sharing and
context-aware compilation approaches schedule concurrent computations or
optimize kernels for resource constraints ([Strati et al.,
2024](#bib.bib22); [Liu et al., 2022](#bib.bib11)). LLM-guided
optimization typically targets individual kernels or programs ([Ouyang
et al., 2025](#bib.bib8); [Dong et al., 2026](#bib.bib9)). Conjugate
Compilation searches joint draft–target compilation plans using an LLM
agent and MCTS, with measurements of the complete co-located shared
execution guiding subsequent proposals. Additional related work is in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Additional Related Work ‣ Conjugate Compilation: Self-Improving Agentic Co-Compilation for Speculative Decoding").

## 6 Conclusion

We introduced Conjugate Compilation, a framework for jointly optimizing
co-located models using the measured performance of their shared
execution to guide compilation. Our approach combines a self-improving
LLM–MCTS search over cross-model compilation plans with transformations
that bring work from multiple models into common kernels, spanning GEMM
and non-GEMM operations. Across speculative-decoding workloads, our
results show that cross-model Conjugate Compilation can recover GPU
capacity left stranded when models are optimized independently.

## Appendix A Additional Search Details

### A.1 Expanding the Search Space

A fixed search space and tuning budget can cause exploration to stall
before profitable co-optimizations are found. When measured progress
plateaus, it first retunes near-miss kernels with progressively larger
budgets, then widens the candidate groups and legal placements, refines
the co-schedule down to individual projection stages so joint nodes can
contain more precisely selected work, and finally exposes additional
kernel-template choices. The agent is informed whenever the available
search space expands.

We impose an effort floor before concluding that a kernel family offers
no profitable co-optimization. Each kernel family from both models is
explored through multiple groupings, and every cross-model GEMM kernel
appearing in a final candidate receives at least the combined tuning
budget of the kernels it replaces. This prevents differences in compiler
effort from determining the comparison.

### A.2 Final Plan Acceptance

Search-time measurements guide exploration, but the reported plan is
subjected to a stricter final evaluation. After search, we re-time the
strongest candidates and the best agent-proposed plans in paired ABBA
blocks against the co-scheduling anchor, while re-measuring the
conventional and plain two-stream baselines in the same measurement
window. A plan is accepted only if its outputs pass the numerical checks
in
§[3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Learning from Compiler and GPU Feedback ‣ 3 Self-Improving Agentic Search for Conjugate Compilation ‣ Conjugate Compilation: Self-Improving Agentic Co-Compilation for Speculative Decoding"),
the paired $`95\%`$ confidence interval of its gain excludes zero, and
its improvement exceeds a noise threshold. Finally, we remove every
range of joint nodes whose leave-one-out gain does not exceed the noise
threshold, unless the full plan is itself significantly faster than the
reduced plan. This final pruning requires each retained range to
contribute measurable value, either independently or through its
interaction with the rest of the plan.

## Appendix B Additional Related Work

### B.1 Parallel Speculative Decoding

Prior work on parallel speculative decoding primarily treats
draft–target parallelism as a runtime scheduling, placement, or
resource-allocation problem rather than a compile-time optimization
problem. Most of these systems place the draft and target models on
separate GPUs, which improve performance but leave both devices
underutilized: PEARL overlaps drafting and verification through adaptive
parallel speculative decoding ([Liu et al., 2025](#bib.bib20)),
MineDraft exploits batch-level parallelism across speculative-decoding
rounds ([Tang et al., 2026c](#bib.bib18)), and SwiftSpec disaggregates
draft and target execution across GPU resources ([Zhang et al.,
2026](#bib.bib19)). FASER instead is a profile-driven runtime system for
parallel draft–target execution on a single GPU, using offline profiling
to guide how the two models share the device ([Chen et al.,
2026](#bib.bib17)). These approaches operate after compilation: they
schedule, place, or partition already-compiled model computations rather
than changing how the draft and target are compiled together. Conjugate
Compilation instead treats co-located execution as a compilation
problem, searching cross-model transformations and evaluating each
candidate using the complete score produced by the co-located shared
execution.

### B.2 GPU Sharing and Context-Aware Compilation

GPU sharing and inter-operator scheduling systems improve utilization by
concurrently executing independent operators or models on the same
device ([Kwon et al., 2020](#bib.bib16); [Ding et al.,
2021](#bib.bib33); [Chen et al., 2025](#bib.bib15); [Strati et al.,
2024](#bib.bib22); [Zhang et al., 2025](#bib.bib14); [Coppock et al.,
2025](#bib.bib21)). These systems do not target speculative decoding;
rather, they address general multi-tenant or inter-operator GPU sharing,
primarily deciding which already-compiled computations should execute
concurrently, while context-aware compilation approaches optimize
individual kernels for a predefined resource budget or interference
condition ([Liu et al., 2022](#bib.bib11); [Ma et al.,
2024](#bib.bib10); [Lu et al., 2025](#bib.bib12)). More fundamentally,
these approaches rely on profiling, analytical models, or conventional
optimization policies to make their scheduling and compilation
decisions; they do not use an execution-driven agentic compiler that
learns how to improve co-located execution from the outcomes of its own
optimization attempts. Conjugate Compilation instead targets speculative
decoding directly and couples an LLM agent with Monte Carlo tree search
to iteratively propose cross-model compilation plans, compile and
execute them, and use the complete score from the co-located shared
execution together with kernel-level verdicts and compiler feedback to
guide subsequent proposals. This self-improving loop makes the measured
behavior of the co-located execution the feedback signal for
compilation, allowing the search to adapt its transformations as it
discovers which optimizations help—or hurt—the models when they execute
together.

### B.3 LLMs For Compiler Optimization

Recent work uses LLMs and agents to optimize individual kernels or
programs ([Ouyang et al., 2025](#bib.bib8); [Dong et al.,
2026](#bib.bib9); [Cummins et al., 2025](#bib.bib34); [Cummins et al.,
2023](#bib.bib35); [Tang et al., 2025](#bib.bib29); [Tang et al.,
2026a](#bib.bib30); [Tang et al., 2026b](#bib.bib31)). Conjugate
Compilation extends agentic optimization across multiple models, using
an LLM with MCTS to search joint draft–target compilation plans guided
by measurements from their co-located shared execution.

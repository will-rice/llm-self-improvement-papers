###### Abstract

Recursively self-improving (RSI) systems are increasingly viewed as a
promising means of accelerating progress toward artificial general
intelligence. They aim to autonomously improve their capabilities by
refining their working pipelines and recursively improving the
strategies that guide subsequent refinements, with gains generalizing to
unseen scenarios. However, existing evaluations lack reliable metrics to
distinguish capability gains from scenario fitting, sustainable and
extensible protocols for unseen tasks and difficulties, and fine-grained
attribution of improvement to evolution traces.

To this end, we propose SelfImprove-Bench, an RSI evaluation framework
that organizes tasks into three primary capabilities (i.e., Exploring,
Planning, Reasoning) as scenarios for guiding model evolution. We
instantiated the tasks on games for controllability and reproducibility,
and pairs them with scalable generation and orchestration driven by task
templates and difficulty coefficients. Beyond task-level scores,
SelfImprove-Bench organizes evaluation into generalization ladder levels
G0 to G4 that measure how far gains transfer from training conditions.
Experiments reveal that the evolutionary gains of existing RSI methods
are inconsistent across generalization levels.

## 1  Introduction

Despite rapid advances in large language models (LLMs), conventional
development still relies heavily on static datasets and offline
training, limiting continuous adaptation to changing environments.
Recursive self-improving (RSI) agents address this limitation by
iteratively modifying their memories, skills, and harnesses using
experience and feedback \[[1](#bib.bib19), [2](#bib.bib16),
[3](#bib.bib18)\]. A central question is whether these improvements
generalize beyond encountered instances to harder tasks, different task
types, and ultimately distinct capabilities. Evaluating this progression
is essential to understanding RSI as a path toward artificial general
intelligence.

Existing benchmarks leave three challenges unresolved
(Table [1](#S2.T1 "Table 1 ‣ 2 Related Work ‣ SelfImprove-Bench: Evaluating Generalization in Self-Improving Agents")).
(i) Systematic generalization assessment. RSI benchmarks examine
selected settings, such as cross-task or cross-difficulty
transfer \[[4](#bib.bib2), [5](#bib.bib29), [6](#bib.bib27)\], but lack
a unified taxonomy spanning instances, difficulty levels, tasks, and
capabilities. (ii) Scalable RSI evaluation. Although some reinforcement
learning benchmarks cover broader generalization
settings \[[7](#bib.bib11)\], they lack a unified protocol for
general-purpose RSI methods. Fixed task pools and limited difficulty
ranges further constrain sustained evaluation as agents evolve and
approach performance ceilings \[[8](#bib.bib8), [9](#bib.bib21)\]. (iii)
Fine-grained process diagnosis. Existing analyses rely primarily on
aggregate metrics, selected case studies, or persistence
ablations \[[10](#bib.bib3), [11](#bib.bib20)\], leaving the
relationship between evolution trajectories and generalization outcomes
insufficiently understood.

To address these challenges, we introduce SelfImprove-Bench, an RSI
evaluation framework combining capability-oriented tasks with a
generalization ladder. (i) The ladder assesses progressively broader
generalization across tasks targeting planning, reasoning, and
exploration. It distinguishes five levels: replay performance on the
same instances (G0), unseen instances at the same difficulty (G1), new
difficulty levels within the same task (G2), different tasks within the
same capability category (G3), and tasks across capability categories
(G4). (ii) A scalable task generation and orchestration module generates
instances using task templates and difficulty coefficients, then
assembles sequences with configurable task types, counts, difficulty
levels, and replay frequencies. (iii) An agent-driven, fine-grained
trace analysis module characterizes refinement semantics, modification
scope, capability dimensions, and generalizability to reveal
method-specific evolution patterns.

Using SelfImprove-Bench, we evaluate three representative classes of RSI
methods and observe substantial variation across the generalization
ladder, including ranking reversals on harder instances and different
task types. Note we focus on non-training RSI methods, whose explicit
modifications to prompts, memories, skills, and harnesses support a
unified evaluation protocol and interpretable evolution traces. Their
lower computational requirements also make repeated evaluation more
tractable. Experiments across three representative classes of RSI
methods reveal substantial differences along the generalization ladder,
including ranking reversals on harder instances and different tasks. As
illustrated in
Figure [1](#S0.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ SelfImprove-Bench: Evaluating Generalization in Self-Improving Agents"),
similar final scores can therefore conceal distinct evolution patterns
and transfer ability.

Our main contributions are as follows.

\(1\) We introduce a capability-oriented generalization ladder for
systematically evaluating RSI across instances, difficulty levels,
tasks, and capabilities.

\(2\) We develop configurable task generation and orchestration to
support scalable evaluation across the ladder.

\(3\) We evaluate representative RSI methods and analyze their evolution
traces, revealing differences in improvement effectiveness,
generalization, and modification patterns.

### 2.1  RSI Methods

We classify RSI methods into three non-exclusive strategies based on
their modification targets:

- •
  Experience-level RSI adapts prompts, memories, and playbooks, as in
  Reflexion, A-MEM, and ACE \[[19](#bib.bib13), [20](#bib.bib14),
  [2](#bib.bib16)\].
- •
  Harness-level RSI modifies tools, workflows, and executable code, as
  in SICA, Self-Harness, and AHE \[[21](#bib.bib9), [22](#bib.bib17),
  [3](#bib.bib18)\].
- •
  Meta-level RSI refines candidate generation or selection, as in DGM’s
  evolving self-modification agent and HyperAgents’ editable meta-agent
  \[[23](#bib.bib10), [1](#bib.bib19)\].

Other strategies include reinforcement learning (RL), which typically
updates policies using reward feedback within a predefined optimization
framework \[[24](#bib.bib35)\]. Data synthesis provides another
strategy, seeking to improve model capabilities through generated
training data \[[25](#bib.bib34)\].

RSI methods flexibly integrate and organize these strategies into a
continual self-improvement process and has been explored as a potential
route toward artificial general intelligence (AGI) \[[26](#bib.bib36)\].
RSI evaluation should therefore examine whether improvements achieved
through refinement transfer to harder tasks and unseen task types within
and across capability categories.

### 2.2  RSI Relevant Benchmarks

RSI-Specific Generalization Evaluation. Common practices include
cross-task testing without explicit capability categories organizing
refinement and transfer \[[4](#bib.bib2), [27](#bib.bib5)\], and
refinement and evaluation over mixed task streams \[[5](#bib.bib29),
[6](#bib.bib27), [28](#bib.bib7), [11](#bib.bib20)\]. Mixed-stream
evaluation examines cumulative performance and experience reuse, but
alone does not isolate source-to-target transfer. Capability-aware
protocols often focus on related-task or within-domain generalization
\[[12](#bib.bib22), [9](#bib.bib21), [16](#bib.bib32)\]. Capability
breakdowns may also serve post-hoc scoring rather than organize
refinement and transfer \[[6](#bib.bib27)\]. Method-specific evaluations
additionally demonstrate cross-domain transfer, as in ADAS
\[[15](#bib.bib31)\].

Difficulty-based task organization does not necessarily include testing
whether gains acquired on easier tasks transfer to harder ones
\[[13](#bib.bib4)\]. Outside agent RSI, Reasoning Gym provides
model-level evaluations of within-domain and cross-domain transfer,
alongside difficulty-based evaluations \[[7](#bib.bib11)\]. A unified
evaluation of agent RSI that combines capability-based refinement,
difficulty generalization, and transfer across task categories remains
underexplored.

RSI-Specific Trace Level Analysis. Process analysis commonly illustrates
refinement through selected modification cases \[[29](#bib.bib6),
[18](#bib.bib33)\]. Quantitative approaches assess experience
contributions through persistence ablations and memory-free comparisons
\[[8](#bib.bib8), [27](#bib.bib5)\], or track capability changes across
refinement checkpoints \[[12](#bib.bib22)\]. Finer analyses can depend
on specific artifact formats: SkillLearnBench measures skill quality,
usage, and alignment with execution trajectories, requiring explicit
skill artifacts \[[10](#bib.bib3)\]. Related work also aggregates
semantic categories of code modifications and relates them to
performance, as in AI4AI-Bench \[[17](#bib.bib1)\]. However, statistical
analysis jointly relating generalization performance, modification
types, target capabilities, and the semantic content of individual
changes remains underexplored.

Tested Tasks. Existing RSI evaluations involve tasks like software
engineering, terminal interaction, web automation, expert-level
reasoning \[[30](#bib.bib23), [31](#bib.bib24), [32](#bib.bib25),
[33](#bib.bib26), [4](#bib.bib2)\], or construct domain-specific tasks
for training-algorithm design and financial workflows \[[17](#bib.bib1),
[6](#bib.bib27)\]. However, end-to-end task scores offer limited
capability-specific diagnosis, while fixed task pools may lose
discriminative power as methods approach score ceilings.

### 3.1  Evolution Protocol

##### Definition.

Following the definition of RSI in  \[[34](#bib.bib28)\], we call a
refinement gain self-improvement to the extent that it persists on
instances, difficulties, and tasks never exposed during refinement, and
task fitting to the extent that it is confined to the training
instances.

##### Task construction and orchestration.

Each instance $`\tau=G_{g}(d,\xi)`$ is generated from game $`g`$,
difficulty $`d`$ , and seed $`\xi`$. The configuration $`\theta`$ first
specify the games to include, then assigns difficulty levels and random
seeds to generate their instances. It specifies how many instances enter
the held-in and held-out sequences and in order, yielding
$`\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{in}}=(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{N})`$ for execution
and refinement and
$`\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{out}}=(\tilde{\tau}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{\tau}_{M})`$
for evaluation. Replay settings determine which held-in tasks can recur,
how frequently, and how far back they are selected, counting only
non-replay tasks. Replays do not trigger refinement. $`N`$ includes
replays. Detailed insertion rules are provided in
Appendix [A.2](#A1.SS2 "A.2 Replay Protocol ‣ Appendix A Evaluation Protocol Details ‣ SelfImprove-Bench: Evaluating Generalization in Self-Improving Agents").
The corresponding task collections $`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{in}}`$ and
$`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{out}}`$ omit order and replay multiplicity. The
generator constructs the held-out pool first and rejects duplicate
task-descriptor hashes, excluding task_id, when generating non-replay
held-in tasks
(Appendix [A.1](#A1.SS1 "A.1 Task Configurations ‣ Appendix A Evaluation Protocol Details ‣ SelfImprove-Bench: Evaluating Generalization in Self-Improving Agents")).
Game categorization is described in
Section [3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Task Categorization ‣ 3 SelfImprove-Bench ‣ SelfImprove-Bench: Evaluating Generalization in Self-Improving Agents").
Task configurations are provided in
Appendix [A.1](#A1.SS1 "A.1 Task Configurations ‣ Appendix A Evaluation Protocol Details ‣ SelfImprove-Bench: Evaluating Generalization in Self-Improving Agents").

We instantiate the framework on games, which satisfy the controllability
and reproducibility requirements of RSI evaluation. Our game rules are
explicit and state spaces are bounded, making it feasible to map
planning, reasoning, and exploring onto different games and avoid the
confounding of multiple abilities that complicates real-world tasks.
Games inherently embody core characteristics of real-world problems such
as partial observability, resource constraints, and multi-step decision
making. Separating self-improvement from task fitting requires fresh
instances and controlled difficulty, which games most readily provide.
We use them as a controlled testbed that complements, rather than
replaces, real-world RSI benchmarks. In addition, these games are agent
tasks mainly based on text interaction and are related to the NLP
community.

##### Agent initialization.

For each RSI method, the initial task agent is $`A_{0}=(B,H_{0})`$,
comprising a common backbone model $`B`$ and a method-specific initial
harness $`H_{0}`$. Backbone settings are reported in
Section [4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Experimental Setup ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ SelfImprove-Bench: Evaluating Generalization in Self-Improving Agents").

##### Refinement and selection.

At step $`t`$, the current agent $`A_{t-1}=(B,H_{t-1})`$ executes
$`\tau_{t}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{in}}`$ through the game’s action
interface, producing an interaction trace and task score. Each task
execution, except replays, is followed by refinement using
benchmark-provided feedback. During refinement, the method may use tools
to inspect and modify its designated workspace, but may not access
SelfImprove-Bench modules or directly access, execute, or evaluate task
instances. Feedback is supplied only through the prescribed interface
(Appendix [A.3](#A1.SS3 "A.3 Feedback Specification ‣ Appendix A Evaluation Protocol Details ‣ SelfImprove-Bench: Evaluating Generalization in Self-Improving Agents")).
When the method proposes candidate harnesses, SelfImprove-Bench
evaluates their agents on the same task instance $`\tau_{t}`$ and
supplies the results, together with the current agent’s result, to the
method’s selection procedure. Selection follows the method-specific rule
and may retain the current harness. The resulting agent
$`A_{t}=(B,H_{t})`$ proceeds to the next task. Candidate validation uses
held-in tasks only, without accessing $`\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{out}}`$.
The backbone $`B`$ remains fixed, and refinement ends when
$`\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{in}}`$ is completed.

##### Evaluation and analysis.

Evaluation uses saved system states and task results according to the
protocols in
Section [3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Generalization Ladder ‣ 3 SelfImprove-Bench ‣ SelfImprove-Bench: Evaluating Generalization in Self-Improving Agents").
Process analysis extracts individual modification proposals from saved
refinement reports and edit records, then annotates and aggregates them
using the criteria in
Section [3.4](#S3.SS4 "3.4 Semantic Process Analysis ‣ 3 SelfImprove-Bench ‣ SelfImprove-Bench: Evaluating Generalization in Self-Improving Agents").
Annotation details are provided in
Appendix [A.4](#A1.SS4 "A.4 Annotation Setup and Validation ‣ Appendix A Evaluation Protocol Details ‣ SelfImprove-Bench: Evaluating Generalization in Self-Improving Agents").

### 3.2  Task Categorization

For rule-constrained tasks with fixed action interfaces, we distinguish
three interacting capabilities: Exploration, acquiring task-relevant
information through observation and interaction. Reasoning, interpreting
rules, deriving implications, and performing computations. Planning,
organizing action sequences to achieve goals under constraints. Games
are categorized by their primary capability demands, providing context
for analyzing modification targets.

### 3.3  Generalization Ladder

The generalization ladder evaluates refinement gain, agent transfer, and
refinement-mechanism effectiveness across G0–G4.

G0: Replay performance. For replay event $`j`$, let
$`A_{\mathrm{init},j}`$ denote the agent before its first execution of
the replayed task, and $`A_{\mathrm{replay},j}`$ the agent executing the
current replay. At G0, compare $`A_{\mathrm{replay},j}`$ with
$`A_{\mathrm{init},j}`$ on the replayed task $`\tau_{j}`$.

G1–G4: Agent generalization. Relative to a source game $`g`$ at
difficulty $`d_{0}`$, $`\mathcal{D}_{1}`$ contains instances
$`G_{g}(d_{0},\xi^{\prime})`$ and $`\mathcal{D}_{2}`$ contains
$`G_{g}(d_{1},\xi^{\prime})`$ with $`d_{1}>d_{0}`$. Collections
$`\mathcal{D}_{1}`$ and $`\mathcal{D}_{2}`$ are drawn from
$`\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{out}}`$, whose task descriptors are kept distinct
from non-replay held-in descriptors by the construction protocol.
Collections $`\mathcal{D}_{3}`$ and $`\mathcal{D}_{4}`$ contain other
games $`g^{\prime}`$ not used for refinement, with
$`c(g^{\prime})=c(g)`$ and $`c(g^{\prime})\neq c(g)`$, respectively.
$`c(g)`$ denotes the primary capability category. For mixed-source
sequences, source games and capability categories are specified for each
transfer comparison. At G1–G4, compare $`A_{T}`$ with $`A_{0}`$ on the
same target instances.

##### Metrics.

Across G0–G4, we use the HCI (Headroom-Closed
Index) \[[34](#bib.bib28)\] to normalize gains relative to the initial
gap to a reference score. We first average the baseline and comparison
scores over target instances, then compute HCI from the resulting means.
Let $`s_{b}`$ and $`s_{a}`$ denote these mean baseline and comparison
scores and $`s_{r}`$ the reference score :

| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

       ``` math
       \mathrm{HCI}=\frac{s_{a}-s_{b}}{s_{r}-s_{b}}.

### 3.4  Semantic Process Analysis

##### Semantic relations.

These indicate whether a proposal introduces new content, contradicts or
revises existing proposals, or duplicates them, relative to the parent
proposal snapshot and earlier proposals in the same stage.

##### Applicability scope.

This distinguishes task-specific proposals, proposals applicable across
tasks sharing a capability, and fully general proposals.

##### Functional targets.

These identify which capability a proposal targets: exploration,
reasoning, planning, or execution. Execution concerns operational
mechanisms such as tool invocation, output formatting, retries, and
error recovery.

##### Modification surfaces.

These identify whether the change affects contextual artifacts, the
execution harness, or the refinement mechanism itself.

## 4  Experiments

We examine the gains from iterative refinement in ACE, AHE, and
HyperAgents and whether these gains extend to unseen instances, harder
tasks, and other games. We further analyze their refinement
trajectories, task scheduling, and execution-harness refinement.

### 4.1  Experimental Setup

##### Tested Baselines.

We evaluate ACE, AHE, and HyperAgents as representative methods of
experience-level, harness-level, and meta-level RSI, respectively
(Section [2](#S2 "2 Related Work ‣ SelfImprove-Bench: Evaluating Generalization in Self-Improving Agents")).
ACE maintains an evolving structured playbook; AHE revises the prompt,
tools, and execution harness based on task feedback; and HyperAgents
uses an improver to modify the task agent’s code repository. We vary the
backbone model, including GPT-5.6 Sol and Claude Opus 5.5, and the
initial execution harness, including Codex and DeepSeek Harness, to
assess how these choices affect RSI performance.

##### Configuration.

Farm, Combat, Sweepmine, and Hero support difficulty levels 1–5. For MC
and Astray, evaluation uses five fixed tasks per game from the official
GameWorld \[[35](#bib.bib15)\] evaluation sets.

### 4.3  RSI Trajectory Analysis

The generalization ladder shows where gains persist; trajectory analysis
examines what the methods change. We use GPT-5.6 Sol to split
improvement artifacts into atomic opinions and annotate their semantic
relations, applicability scope, functional targets, and modification
surfaces.
Table [3](#S4.T3 "Table 3 ‣ 4.2 Main Results ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ SelfImprove-Bench: Evaluating Generalization in Self-Improving Agents")
covers the 915 opinions retained in the final snapshots of the six
games.

Task-specific refinement can coexist with transferable gains.
Task-specific opinions predominate in all three methods;
category-general or general ones make up 14.2% of ACE’s retained
opinions, 7.8% of AHE’s, and 36.5% of HyperAgents’. Yet G3 and G4 show
positive transfer across games, sometimes exceeding direct refinement on
the target.

Refinement emphasizes solving observed problems over exploring
alternatives. Reasoning and planning appear in 61.2% and 49.6% of
retained opinions, versus 11.0% for exploration. Current RSI methods may
rely on observed trajectories to find and fix problems rather than
explore alternatives.

Main-experiment changes concentrate on the task agent. 87.9% of retained
opinions target prompts and memory and 12.1% the execution harness; none
modify the improver or the improvement process.

![Refer to caption](2611.02797v1/agent_comparison_icons.png)

Figure 5: Comparison with general-purpose agents. Medium held-out scores
of general-purpose agents and refined agents, ranked within each game.

More active revision may contribute to better refinement outcomes. ACE,
AHE, and HyperAgents revise 14.2%, 30.6%, and 40.2% of their recorded
opinions, respectively. AHE and HyperAgents revise existing opinions
more frequently and generally achieve better final performance than ACE,
suggesting that effective refinement may depend not only on adding new
opinions but also on continually revising existing strategies.

Frequent revision does not eliminate duplicate opinions. HyperAgents has
the highest revision rate, yet on Hero its duplicate rate reaches 67.6%,
against 46.1% for AHE and 12.8% for ACE.

## 5  Conclusion

We presented SelfImprove-Bench, a scalable benchmark for RSI evaluation.
Different from conventional benchmarks, SelfImprove-Bench supports
template- and difficulty-controlled task generation for configurable
evaluation. We propose a generalization ladder to reflect the transfer
to new instances, new difficulties, new tasks within a capability, and
across capabilities. We evaluate three representative classes of RSI
methods and find that their effectiveness varies across the
generalization ladder, with their relative rankings reversing when
transferring to tasks of higher difficulty or different types.

## Appendix A Evaluation Protocol Details

### A.1  Task Configurations

The task-related fields of $`\theta`$ determine which instances are
generated and how they form $`\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{in}}`$ and
$`\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{out}}`$; model and runtime settings are
configured separately. Game definitions, difficulty parameters, and
scoring rules are provided in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Game Design ‣ SelfImprove-Bench: Evaluating Generalization in Self-Improving Agents").

##### Held-in tasks.

The ordered schedule list contains blocks with game, difficulty, and
count fields. Each block generates the requested number of non-replay
instances of that game and difficulty. Blocks are concatenated in list
order before replay insertion; count excludes inserted replays. The
block-level replay flag controls eligibility for replay insertion, whose
exact semantics are defined in
Appendix [A.2](#A1.SS2 "A.2 Replay Protocol ‣ Appendix A Evaluation Protocol Details ‣ SelfImprove-Bench: Evaluating Generalization in Self-Improving Agents").
The standard ordered setting uses random=false and repeat=false; these
are also the defaults.

##### Held-out tasks.

The ordered holdout list specifies evaluation blocks using the same
game, difficulty, and count fields. For each requested game–difficulty
pair, holdout*tasks_per_difficulty sets the candidate pool size, while
the block’s count selects that many instances from the beginning of its
pool. Selected blocks form $`\mathcal{S}*{\mathrm{out}}`$ in list order,
without replay insertion.

##### Seeds and reconstruction.

The seed and holdout_seed fields control held-in and held-out
generation, respectively. For newly generated instances, task seeds are
derived deterministically from the root seed, phase, game, difficulty,
instance index, and generation attempt using SHA-256. The current
generator rejects duplicate task-payload hashes, excluding task_id,
across the generated held-out pool and non-replay held-in instances;
replay insertion deliberately reuses an existing task. This is
descriptor-level deduplication: seed fields remain in the hashed
payload, so different descriptors are not guaranteed to produce
different underlying game states. Each run saves its resolved
configuration and task manifest as config.resolved.json and
task_manifest.json, including task instances, seeds, sequence order, and
replay links. These artifacts and the corresponding generator version
specify the realized sequences.

### A.2  Replay Protocol

##### Execution.

Each replay executes the original task instance using the current agent,
and records the resulting score for G0 evaluation. Replay executions do
not trigger refinement.

##### Insertion rule.

Replays are inserted while constructing the held-in sequence, before
execution. Let $`u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}`$ be the non-replay tasks placed so
far, with $`u_{n}`$ belonging to the current schedule block. After
appending $`u_{n}`$, insert a replay of $`u_{n-\ell}`$ if the current
block enables replay, the interval $`e`$ and lag $`\ell`$ are positive,
$`n\bmod e=0`$, and $`n>\ell`$. Both interval and lag count only
non-replay tasks, globally across schedule blocks.

##### Boundary conditions.

No replay is inserted when history is insufficient ($`n\leq\ell`$), when
the current block disables replay, or when replay is globally disabled
($`e=\ell=0`$). No additional replay is inserted to complete a partial
interval. The rule also applies after the final non-replay task: a
replay is appended if the same conditions hold. The ordering above
assumes random=false; enabling random shuffles the assembled sequence,
including its replay entries, after insertion.

### A.3  Feedback Specification

##### Abstract interface.

The benchmark passes task execution results to refinement through a
FeedbackResult object,

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathcal{F}=(\mathrm{id},\mathrm{task},\mathrm{trajectory},\mathrm{evaluation}).
``` |  |

The identifier links feedback to its execution, and task contains the
game-specific task descriptor. The ordered trajectory contains step
records with step_index, prompt, image_records, action,
environment_response, success, score, feedback, and an optional
agent_call. The latter retains the model response and, when available,
reasoning, token usage, provider, model, and call metadata. Image
records reference available images; text-only interactions use an empty
image list. Step scores, success flags, and feedback may be absent or
null when the game does not provide them.

The evaluation object contains execution status, task success, the task
score, optional explanatory feedback, and game-specific metrics.
Execution status distinguishes completion, error, timeout, and
cancellation; a completed execution need not solve the task
successfully. Metrics depend on the game and can include consumed
actions or restart counts. Game-specific scoring is described in
Appendix [B](#A2 "Appendix B Game Design ‣ SelfImprove-Bench: Evaluating Generalization in Self-Improving Agents").

### A.4  Annotation Setup and Validation

The analysis records use gpt-5.6-sol for semantic annotation. We checked
100 pre-extracted proposals, stratified by game and method across six
games and three methods, against their source modifications.
Table [7](#A1.T7 "Table 7 ‣ A.4 Annotation Setup and Validation ‣ Appendix A Evaluation Protocol Details ‣ SelfImprove-Bench: Evaluating Generalization in Self-Improving Agents")
reports agreement with human labels after human-approved,
assistant-assisted review with access to machine labels; these are
post-review results rather than independent blind agreement estimates.

|  Measure                     | After review |
|------------------------------|--------------|
|  Scope exact agreement       | 88%          |
|  Surface exact agreement     | 99%          |
|  Function any-label overlap  | 96%          |
|  Function mean Jaccard       | 0.828        |

Table 7: Agreement between fixed machine labels and reviewed human
labels on 100 sampled proposals.

Scope and Surface each take one label. Function permits multiple labels
because a single proposal can affect several functions, such as
reasoning and planning. Complete-set agreement is therefore stricter
than any-label overlap, which requires only
$`H_{i}\cap M_{i}\neq\varnothing`$ for human and machine label sets
$`H_{i}`$ and $`M_{i}`$. Mean Jaccard similarity averages
$`|H_{i}\cap M_{i}|/|H_{i}\cup M_{i}|`$ across proposals, accounting for
both missing and additional labels. The results indicate strong
correspondence after review, particularly for Surface, while differences
remain in Scope and complete Function combinations. The 96% Function
overlap indicates a shared functional label on most proposals, not
complete agreement or classification accuracy.

## Appendix B Game Design

In this appendix, we describe the game design in our dataset and detail
how game instances are generated by combining task templates with
difficulty coefficients.

### B.1  Planning Games

#### B.1.1  Task Definition

Farm. In this game, an agent operates a farm under cash, land, and time
constraints. Each task specifies the initial cash, number of plots,
planning days horizon, and a task-specific crop catalog containing seed
prices, maturation times, harvest yields, and selling prices. The agent
receives the entire instance in one non-interactive call and returns a
JSON plan indexed by day. Within each day, actions are executed in the
fixed order buy, plant, harvest, sell. The objective is to maximize
terminal cash profit, i.e., final cash minus initial cash.

Combat. Combat is a deterministic deck-building game comprising
alternating combats and shops followed by a boss encounter. Player
health, gold, and deck composition persist across steps. At a combat
step, the agent observes its current state, owned cards, the enemy’s
repeating public intent cycle, and the exact deterministic hands. It
returns the cards to play on selected turns subject to hand multiplicity
and energy constraints. At a shop step, the agent may buy each offered
card and purchase one heal, all within its current gold budget. The goal
is to defeat the complete encounter chain, including the boss, while
maximizing an objective that rewards defeated enemies, remaining health,
and remaining gold.

#### B.1.2  Task Generation

Farm. Let $`d`$ denote difficulty. The generator fixes initial cash to
$`200`$, samples $`2d+2`$ crops uniformly without replacement from the
global catalog, and sets both the number of plots and the days horizon
to $`4d+4`$. Thus, increasing $`d`$ simultaneously enlarges the action
vocabulary, land capacity, and temporal planning depth. Crops are not
tiered, and instance variation arises from the sampled combinations of
crop prices, yields, and maturation times.

Combat. For difficulty $`d\in\{1,2,3,4,5\}`$, the available catalog
contains every card of tier at most $`d`$. The initial deck contains a
fixed card set, plus one uniformly sampled card from each tier
$`2,\ldots,d`$. Player parameters remain fixed at $`80`$ maximum and
initial health, $`120`$ initial gold, $`3`$ energy, and $`5`$ drawn
cards per turn. Each combat permits at most $`15`$ turns. For every tier
$`i\leq d`$, the generator samples one non-boss enemy of tier $`i`$ as
the enemy, and then appends a shop. Each shop offers three distinct
cards. One offer is forced to have tier $`\min(d,i+1)`$, while the other
two are sampled from cards at or below that tier. A tier-$`d`$ boss is
at last, producing $`d+1`$ combats and $`2d+1`$ total steps. Enemies and
cards become stronger with tier, while the longer step chain increases
resource coupling across decisions.

#### B.1.3  References Calculation

Planning a game involves complex permutations and combinations, and it
is difficult to directly compute an optimal solution for it. Therefore,
specific search algorithms are used to find a near-optimal solution
within a limited time as a pseudo ground-truth.

Farm. The pseudo ground-truth for each farm game is derived from a
rolling search. On each day, matured batches are first harvested and
sold. Then for each candidate crop, the heuristic tries to greedily
plant it into as many free plots as cash permits, and rolls the
resulting state to the days horizon using the same daily greedy rule.
The candidate with the largest projected terminal cash is committed, and
the pseudo ground-truth profit is the terminal cash minus the initial
cash. In the above search branches, the daily decision is to plant as
much of the same crop as possible, whereas in fact there may be better
strategies such as planting a combination of multiple crops. Therefore,
the ratio (score) of these strategies compared with the pseudo
ground-truth can exceed 1.

Combat. Combat computes its reference in two search levels. For each
combat stage, the solver tries every legal card combination by turns and
simulates its outcome. Among winning plans, the solver prefers higher
remaining health and then fewer turns. At each shop, it tries every
affordable combination of the three offered cards, both with and without
healing. The solver then carries these alternatives through the
following stages. To keep the search tractable, partial runs are ranked
using remaining health, gold, and the damage and block provided by the
deck, and only the top-k plans are continued. After the boss, the
completed run with the highest official objective becomes the reference
candidate.

### B.2  Reasoning Games

#### B.2.1  Task Definition

Hero. We adapt Hero’s Journey \[[36](#bib.bib12)\], a text-based
adventure benchmark for hidden-rule induction. Given demonstrations and
a task description, the agent infers the missing rules and returns a
complete action sequence in one response. The objective is to reach the
task goal within the action budget.

Each benchmark task contains five separately initialized episodes with
different seeds. A successful episode is scored by the ratio of
reference solution length to executed action count; an unsuccessful
episode receives zero. The task score is the mean episode score.

Sweepmine. The agent identifies mines on a partially observed board.
Each revealed safe cell indicates the number of mines among its eight
neighboring cells. At each step, the agent opens, marks, or unmarks a
cell and receives the updated board. The objective is to mark every mine
without marking any safe cell within the action budget. Opening a mine
generates a new board without resetting the remaining budget.

An unsuccessful task receives zero. For a successful task, the score is
$`(B-a)/(B-m)`$, where $`B`$ is the action budget, $`a`$ the number of
actions consumed, and $`m`$ the number of mines.

|  |  |  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|----|----|
| Game | d1 | d2 | d3 | d4 | d5 |
| Hero | Ovr/1/3 | Ord/2/2 | Ord/3/2 | Ord/4/2 | Ord/5/2 |
| Sweepmine |  $`5\times 5`$/ 3/16 |  $`6\times 6`$/ 5/22 |  $`7\times 7`$/ 8/30 |  $`8\times 8`$/ 11/40 |  $`9\times 9`$/ 15/52 |

Table 8: Reasoning game difficulty configurations. Hero entries give
rule template / hidden action blocks / budget multiplier; Sweepmine
entries give board size / mines / action budget.

#### B.2.2  Task Generation

Hero. We generate five episodes per task using different seeds, with
templates and element pools adapted from the official implementation.
Table [8](#A2.T8 "Table 8 ‣ B.2.1 Task Definition ‣ B.2 Reasoning Games ‣ Appendix B Game Design ‣ SelfImprove-Bench: Evaluating Generalization in Self-Improving Agents")
specifies the rule template, hidden block count, and action-budget
multiplier. Override (Ovr) uses class-based defaults with a role-based
exception; Ordered (Ord) requires inferring each block’s action type,
repetition count, and relative order. The action budget is the reference
solution length times the multiplier.

Sweepmine. We generate boards with seed-controlled mine placement.
Difficulty-specific configurations specify the board size as rows
$`\times`$ columns, the total number of mines, and the action budget as
the maximum number of attempted actions across all board restarts
(Table [8](#A2.T8 "Table 8 ‣ B.2.1 Task Definition ‣ B.2 Reasoning Games ‣ Appendix B Game Design ‣ SelfImprove-Bench: Evaluating Generalization in Self-Improving Agents")).
The game rules and action interface remain fixed across levels.

### B.3  Exploring Games

#### B.3.1  Task Definition

MC. Minecraft (MC) is a GameWorld browser-based first-person
resource-collection and environment-exploration task. The agent starts
with an empty inventory and uses visual observations to locate target
resources, move, adjust its view, and interact with blocks to complete a
collection goal within the step budget. Each step provides the task
description, a short interaction history, and the current screenshot,
and the agent returns one JSON action.

Astray. Astray is a GameWorld maze-navigation task in which the agent
uses screenshots to guide a ball to the exit. We adapt the scoring to
use the maximum relative reduction in breadth-first search (BFS)
shortest-path distance to the exit over the run.

#### B.3.2  Task Generation

MC. We define 20 training tasks with different collection goals and
target quantities; evaluation uses the five fixed MC tasks from the
official GameWorld evaluation set.

Astray. Training mazes have side length $`L(d)=2d+9`$ for difficulty
$`d\in\{1,\ldots,5\}`$, with layouts determined by random seeds.
Evaluation uses the five fixed Astray tasks from the official GameWorld

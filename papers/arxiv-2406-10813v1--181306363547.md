###### Abstract

The alignment of large language models (LLMs) is crucial not only for
unlocking their potential in specific tasks but also for ensuring that
responses meet human expectations and adhere to safety and ethical
principles. Current alignment methodologies face considerable
challenges. For instance, supervised fine-tuning (SFT) requires
extensive, high-quality annotated samples, while reinforcement learning
from human feedback (RLHF) is complex and often unstable. In this paper,
we introduce _self-evolution fine-tuning_ (SEFT) for policy
optimization, with the aim of eliminating the need for annotated samples
while retaining the stability and efficiency of SFT. SEFT first trains
an adaptive reviser to elevate low-quality responses while maintaining
high-quality ones. The reviser then gradually guides the policy’s
optimization by fine-tuning it with enhanced responses. One of the
prominent features of this method is its ability to leverage unlimited
amounts of unannotated data for policy optimization through supervised
fine-tuning. Our experiments on AlpacaEval 2.0 and MT-Bench demonstrate
the effectiveness of SEFT. We also provide a comprehensive analysis of
its advantages over existing alignment techniques.

## 1 Introduction

Recent years have showcased the remarkable capabilities and performance
of large language models (LLMs) across a broad range of tasks. These
capabilities are attributed not only to their vast parameter sizes and
the extensive text corpora used for pre-training \[[17](#bib.bib17)\]
but also to the critical process of aligning these models with human
expectations \[[24](#bib.bib24)\]. Such alignment is essential to ensure
that the outputs of LLMs are helpful, honest, and harmless
\[[1](#bib.bib1)\] across various tasks and applications.

The pursuit of aligning LLMs with human preferences has led to three
predominant methodologies, among others: supervised fine-tuning (SFT),
reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) \[[7](#bib.bib7)\],
and offline RLHF. SFT directly fine-tunes LLMs on downstream tasks using
instruction-following data, guiding the models to produce responses that
match the ground truth in the dataset \[[8](#bib.bib8)\]. RLHF offers a
sophisticated approach by initially training a reward model that assigns
higher rewards to responses aligning better with human preferences,
followed by optimizing the policy LLM using policy-gradient methods like
proximal policy optimization (PPO) \[[27](#bib.bib27)\]. In offline
RLHF, exemplified by direct preference optimization (DPO)
\[[25](#bib.bib25)\], the LLM policy is directly optimized using
pre-collected preference data, eliminating the necessity for a reward
model. This approach aims to enhance the probability of producing
_chosen_ responses while reducing the likelihood of _rejected_ ones.

Each of these methods comes with its strengths and weaknesses. SFT,
while efficient, is hindered by the scarcity of high-quality
human-annotated data and tends to suffer from poor adaptability to
out-of-distribution samples \[[19](#bib.bib19)\]. RLHF demands
substantial computational overhead for training an additional reward
model \[[5](#bib.bib5)\] and faces optimization challenges such as
inefficiency and instability. Offline RLHF methods, which are directly
optimized on preference data without the need for a reward model, tend
to suffer from distribution drift issues and may lead to biased policies
that favor out-of-distribution responses \[[36](#bib.bib36)\], thereby
impairing generalization to unseen samples.

In response to these challenges, this paper introduces a novel
_self-evolution fine-tuning_ (SEFT) method for policy optimization. Our
approach first trains a _preliminary reviser_ based on preference data,
which takes a prompt and the raw response of an LLM as input and outputs
a higher-quality response. Such training data is widely available in
various preference datasets and can be easily created from supervised
fine-tuning data. However, our initial experiments suggest that the
preliminary reviser tends to revise the original responses
indiscriminately, regardless of its capabilities, which may have a
counterproductive effect on high-quality responses. As illustrated in
Figure
[1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Self-Evolution Fine-Tuning for Policy Optimization"),
the reviser generally enhances low-quality responses but occasionally
degrades high-quality ones.

Therefore, we continue to train an _adaptive reviser_ that learns to
assign a revision label based on the difficulty of revising the initial
response as evaluated by the preliminary reviser: \[Major Revise\]
indicates a substantial revision, \[Minor Revise\] signifies a minor
revision, and \[No Revise\] means that no revision is needed. The
primary purpose of introducing the revision label is to guide the
reviser to make revisions where feasible and refrain from attempting
those beyond its capability. This ensures the overall quality of the
responses produced by the reviser.

The adaptive reviser can be used to assess the quality of the policy’s
outputs and improve low-quality responses to high-quality ones for
subsequent fine-tuning of the policy. This adaptive mechanism aligns the
policy with human preferences without the need for exhaustive annotated
data or complex explorations required in RLHF, and offers an innovative
self-evolution fine-tuning (SEFT) strategy that balances efficiency and
stability during policy optimization. The rationale behind SEFT is that
it utilizes the effectiveness of pseudo-labels generated by powerful
LLMs for fine-tuning. Various studies \[[4](#bib.bib4)\] have shown that
pseudo-labeling enables models to be fine-tuned with high-quality
synthetic labels created by robust LLMs like GPT-4. These labels
approximate human annotations and allow the policy to be aligned with
human-like responses at minimal cost.

To evaluate the proposed SEFT, we implemented the adaptive reviser on
Nectar \[[42](#bib.bib42)\] using renowned LLMs of various scales,
including OpenChat-3.5-7B \[[33](#bib.bib33)\], Qwen1.5-32B-Chat
\[[3](#bib.bib3)\], and Yi-34B-Chat \[[37](#bib.bib37)\]. We then
performed policy optimization with Zephyr-7B-SFT-full
\[[32](#bib.bib32)\] as the base model on UltraFeedback
\[[9](#bib.bib9)\], where only the prompts were used. The optimized
policy was evaluated on the AlpacaEval 2.0 \[[12](#bib.bib12)\] and
MT-Bench \[[41](#bib.bib41)\] benchmarks. Experimental results
demonstrate the effectiveness of SEFT and highlight its superiority over
traditional alignment methods such as SFT, DPO \[[25](#bib.bib25)\], and
ORPO \[[14](#bib.bib14)\]. The empirical results also reveal that the
proposed adaptive reviser can effectively assess the difficulty of
revising responses and enhance the overall quality of model outputs.

Furthermore, experiments with additional unlabeled data from
OpenHermes-2.5 \[[29](#bib.bib29)\] show that incorporating more
unlabeled data consistently enhances the performance of SEFT. Besides,
ablation studies indicate that incrementally improving the quality of
alignment data during policy optimization leads to better performance
gains than directly applying high-quality alignment data. This further
highlights the rationality of the proposed internal evolution and
external evolution in SEFT.

## 2 Related Work

As large language models (LLMs) continue to advance and grow in
capability, aligning them with human expectations and values becomes
increasingly urgent and complex. In this section, we briefly review the
mainstream methods for aligning LLMs and highlight how our approach
differs.

### 2.1 Alignment Methods

#### SFT

Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) bridges the gap between the pre-training
objective of language modeling in LLMs and the adaptation objective of
making LLMs follow human instructions \[[39](#bib.bib39)\].
Representative SFT approaches with instruction-following data are as
follows. Flan-T5 \[[8](#bib.bib8)\] leverages Flan instruction-tuning
data \[[23](#bib.bib23)\] on a series of T5 models \[[26](#bib.bib26)\],
achieving improved few-shot performance. Vicuna \[[6](#bib.bib6)\]
utilizes a dataset of 70K user-ChatGPT dialogues from ShareGPT¹¹ 1
https://sharegpt.com/ and is built on the Llama-1-13B model
\[[30](#bib.bib30)\]. It was claimed to achieve performance comparable
to larger and powerful LLMs. UltraLM \[[9](#bib.bib9)\] fine-tunes the
Llama2-13b model \[[31](#bib.bib31)\] with UltraChat 200K instances
\[[10](#bib.bib10)\], once achieved the top-1 rank on the AlpacaEval
leaderboard \[[21](#bib.bib21)\]. These works illustrate the efficacy of
using annotated instruction-tuning data to fine-tune LLMs for alignment.

#### RLHF

Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) has emerged as a
powerful method for effectively aligning LLMs by incorporating human
feedback into the learning process. This approach relies on substantial
datasets of human preferences to train a reward model, which evaluates
policy responses and guides the optimization process. As a pioneering
work, the integration of proximal policy optimization (PPO)
\[[27](#bib.bib27)\] for RLHF has led to notable successes in advanced
models like InstructGPT \[[24](#bib.bib24)\]. Moreover, initiatives like
RLAIF \[[20](#bib.bib20)\] aim to address the scarcity of human
preference data by generating synthetic datasets (e.g., UltraFeedback
\[[9](#bib.bib9)\] and Nectar \[[42](#bib.bib42)\]) using super models
like GPT-4. Besides, several works try to improve RLHF by mitigating its
inherent challenges. For example, APA \[[43](#bib.bib43)\] employs a
squared error loss function that incorporates estimated advantages,
providing stable control over policy deviations and preventing mode
collapse.

#### Offline RLHF

Offline RLHF methods like DPO \[[25](#bib.bib25)\] employ the idea of
contrastive learning to avoid the construction of reward models as well
as the complex process of reinforcement learning. Representative works
in this line also include SLiC-HF \[[40](#bib.bib40)\], which aligns
model outputs with human preferences by incorporating two losses:
calibration loss adjusts the model by increasing the likelihood of
generating positive responses relative to negative ones, while
regularization loss discourages the model from deviating too far from
the reference model. IPO \[[2](#bib.bib2)\], as part of a general
framework that includes RLHF and DPO, directly optimizes a pairwise
preference objective with KL regularization to maintain policy alignment
with a reference policy, thereby avoiding overfitting associated with
traditional models that rely on pointwise reward substitution.
Furthermore, KTO \[[13](#bib.bib13)\] introduces a simple binary signal
to determine the desirability of outputs, eliminating the need for
expensive preference data and offering a cost-effective solution for
real-world applications.

#### Rejection Sampling

Rejection sampling is a sample generation technique that produces
candidate samples from a proposal distribution and then decides whether
to accept or reject each candidate based on a certain criterion. In the
context of LLM alignment, rejection sampling is often employed for
efficient data augmentation. This process generates multiple responses
for each prompt and then accepts or rejects these responses based on
scores assigned by reward models or human evaluators. The
highest-scoring responses are collected for supervised fine-tuning, a
process also known as Best-of-N sampling \[[31](#bib.bib31)\]. Moreover,
these pairs of responses consisting of high-scoring and low-scoring
candidates can also be gathered as preference data for alignment
training \[[22](#bib.bib22), [18](#bib.bib18)\].

### 2.2 Discussions

The proposed SEFT method differs from previous approaches by focusing
primarily on optimizing policy through progressively revised responses,
achieved by training an adaptive reviser on existing preference data.
This mechanism enables the alignment of LLMs without the need for
extensive data annotations or complex explorations as required by SFT or
RLHF. With the adaptive reviser, we can fine-tune the policy using
unlimited amounts of unannotated instruction data. This self-evolution
process ensures both efficiency and stability during policy
optimization.

In the context of revising LLM outputs, Ji et al. \[[15](#bib.bib15)\]
introduced a method called Aligner, which employs a smaller LLM to
refine the outputs of a primary LLM, mainly aiming to enhance the
responses’ usefulness and safety. Unlike Aligner, our SEFT focuses on
optimizing policy models through a supervised fine-tuning process using
progressively enhanced responses. Moreover, Aligner requires specific
training data that involves GPT-4 for annotations, whereas SEFT utilizes
existing preference or supervised fine-tuning data, reducing the need
for data annotations and simplifying the process. Lastly, while Aligner
enforces the revision of a response, SEFT introduces an adaptive
strategy that first evaluates the difficulty of revising the response
and then decides whether to revise it.

![Refer to caption](2406.10813v1/overview_0611.png)

Figure 2: Overview of SEFT. The reviser takes prompts and initial
responses of varying quality as input, assesses the difficulty of
revising these responses, and assigns appropriate revision labels to
generate overall high-quality responses. During policy optimization, the
policy first undergoes internal evolution: the reviser revises the
responses generated by the policy and uses them to fine-tune the policy.
Then, the policy undergoes external evolution with a stronger model,
progressively enhancing the quality of alignment data and guiding the
policy toward generating better responses.

## 3 SEFT: Self-Evolution Fine-Tuning

Self-Evolution Fine-Tuning (SEFT) aims to provide a robust and efficient
solution for policy optimization. To achieve this, as shown in Figure
[2](#S2.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 2.2 Discussions ‣ 2 Related Work ‣ Self-Evolution Fine-Tuning for Policy Optimization"),
we first train an adaptive reviser that evaluates the initial responses
generated by the policy and makes appropriate revisions to enhance the
overall quality. Then, the policy is fine-tuned using these enhanced
responses as pseudo-labels. This self-evolution mechanism allows us to
optimize the policy without exhaustive annotated data or complex
explorations while maintaining the stability and efficiency of
supervised fine-tuning (SFT). Below, we first provide an overview of the
SEFT method in Section
[3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Overview ‣ 3 SEFT: Self-Evolution Fine-Tuning ‣ Self-Evolution Fine-Tuning for Policy Optimization").
We then give a detailed explanation of the training process for the
adaptive reviser in Section
[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Reviser Training ‣ 3 SEFT: Self-Evolution Fine-Tuning ‣ Self-Evolution Fine-Tuning for Policy Optimization"),
followed by the policy optimization using the reviser in Section
[3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Alignment ‣ 3 SEFT: Self-Evolution Fine-Tuning ‣ Self-Evolution Fine-Tuning for Policy Optimization").

### 3.1 Overview

As depicted in Figure
[3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3.2 Reviser Training ‣ 3 SEFT: Self-Evolution Fine-Tuning ‣ Self-Evolution Fine-Tuning for Policy Optimization"),
the proposed SEFT begins with training an adaptive reviser
$`\mathcal{R}`$ using prompts (instructions) and responses of varying
quality. For each prompt, a pair of responses is provided, one denoting
a low-quality response and the other a high-quality response. Such
training data is broadly available in various preference datasets
\[[9](#bib.bib9)\] and can also be easily created from supervised
fine-tuning data. In preference datasets, for example, the low-quality
response and high-quality response correspond to _reject_ and _chosen_
responses, respectively. The training of the reviser starts from a
strong base model $`\mathcal{M}`$ and involves an initial warm-up phase
to create a preliminary reviser, followed by adaptive training to
continually refine the reviser. This enables the reviser to adaptively
revise a response based on the difficulty of the revision and minimize
misrevisions.

We then apply the reviser twice for policy optimization on unlabeled
prompts. First, initial responses for these prompts are sampled from the
policy $`\mathcal{P}`$, and the reviser is applied to refine these
responses. These revised responses serve as pseudo-labels to fine-tune
the policy. Next, the base model $`\mathcal{M}`$ is employed to generate
another set of initial responses for these prompts, which are also
refined by the reviser $`\mathcal{R}`$ to enhance their quality. These
enhanced responses are then used to fine-tune the policy once more. This
two-phase approach forms an internal-external evolution path, which
allows us to first fine-tune $`\mathcal{P}`$ within its own response
space before expanding to a more challenging response space, promoting
progressive improvements. This process also follows the idea of
curriculum learning \[[34](#bib.bib34)\].

One might naturally expect an iterative application of the reviser to
optimize the policy, but this proves to be infeasible. This is because
the reviser does not perform iterative training but directly refines
initial responses into final responses, preventing gradual improvement
over multiple iterations. Our experiments also confirm that the policy
does not benefit from the second round of optimization.

### 3.2 Reviser Training

Given a revision dataset $`\mathcal{D}^{r}=\{X^{r},{Y^{l}},{Y^{h}}\}`$,
where $`X^{r}=\{X^{r}_{i}\}|_{i=1}^{N}`$ stands for a set of prompts,
$`Y^{l}=\{Y_{i}^{l}\}|_{i=1}^{N}`$ denotes the original low-quality
responses for these prompts, and $`Y^{h}=\{Y_{i}^{h}\}|_{i=1}^{N}`$ are
the corresponding responses of higher quality. For simplicity, this work
leverages an existing preference dataset for $`\mathcal{D}^{r}`$, where
_rejected_ responses correspond to $`Y^{l}`$ and _chosen_ responses
correspond to $`Y^{h}`$. We partition $`\mathcal{D}^{r}`$ into two
distinct splits
$`\mathcal{D}^{r}=\{\mathcal{D}_{1}^{r},\mathcal{D}_{2}^{r}\}`$ for
warm-up training and adaptive training, respectively. The overall
training objective of the reviser $`\mathcal{R}`$ is to assess the level
of difficulty in revising $`{Y}^{l}`$ to $`{Y}^{h}`$ and implement
adaptive revisions.

![Refer to caption](2406.10813v1/reviser_figure_final.png)

Figure 3: Illustrative training examples for the adaptive reviser. The
objective of the adaptive reviser is to make revisions where feasible
and avoid attempting those beyond its capabilities.

#### Warm-up Training

We begin with warm-up training on $`\mathcal{D}_{1}^{r}`$ to obtain a
preliminary reviser $`\hat{\mathcal{R}}`$, aiming to transform the
low-quality responses into high-quality responses. This process can be
represented as
$`\hat{\mathcal{R}}:X^{r}\times Y^{l}\rightarrow{Y}^{h}`$. Specifically,
the training objective is to minimize the negative log-likelihood (NLL)
of high-quality responses $`{Y}^{h}`$ given prompts $`{X^{r}}`$ and
low-quality responses $`{Y}^{l}`$:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|     |

````math
\mathcal{L}_{r}=-\mathbb{E}_{(X_{i}^{r},Y_{i}^{l},Y_{i}^{h})\sim\mathcal{D}_{1}^{r}}\left[\mathrm{log}P_{\theta}(Y_{i}^{h}|X_{i}^{r},Y_{i}^{l})\right],
``` |  | (1) |

where $`\theta`$ refers to the parameters of the reviser
$`\hat{\mathcal{R}}`$ initialized from the base model $`\mathcal{M}`$.
Note that this training process enables the reviser to utilize
information from both the prompts in $`X^{r}`$ and the initial responses
in $`Y^{l}`$ to generate the final responses $`Y^{h}`$.

#### Adaptive Training

The above preliminary reviser $`\hat{\mathcal{R}}`$ tends to revise the
original responses indiscriminately, regardless of its capabilities,
which may lead to the deterioration of high-quality responses, as
illustrated in Figure
[1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ Self-Evolution Fine-Tuning for Policy Optimization").
Ideally, we want the reviser to make revisions where feasible and avoid
attempting those beyond its capability. To achieve this, we define three
labels based on the difficulty of revising the initial responses as
evaluated by the preliminary reviser $`\hat{\mathcal{R}}`$: \[Major
Revise\], \[Minor Revise\], and \[No Revise\]. \[Major Revise\]
indicates a substantial revision, \[Minor Revise\] signifies a minor
revision, and \[No Revise\] means no revision is needed.

Since the original training dataset lacks revision labels, an intuitive
approach is to use human labor for annotations. However, this method is
not only expensive but may also introduce potential biases from human
annotators \[[28](#bib.bib28)\]. To automatically obtain revision
labels, we apply the preliminary reviser $`\hat{\mathcal{R}}`$ on
$`\mathcal{D}_{2}^{r}`$ and evaluate its revisions as follows. For each
sample in $`\mathcal{D}_{2}^{r}`$ consisting of a prompt $`X_{i}^{r}`$
and the original response $`{Y}_{i}^{l}`$, we first apply
$`\hat{\mathcal{R}}`$ to revise $`{Y}_{i}^{l}`$. We then use an
off-the-shelf critic model $`\mathcal{C}`$ to assess the revised
response $`\hat{Y}_{i}^{l}`$ against the original response
$`Y_{i}^{l}`$, assigning a benefit score $`s_{i}`$ for the revision to
measure the extent of improvement achieved by the preliminary reviser.
We compare the reward $`s_{i}`$ with two thresholds, $`\delta_{l}`$ and
$`\delta_{h}`$, to assign an appropriate revision label $`r_{i}`$ for
the prompt $`X_{i}^{r}`$. If the reward $`s_{i}`$ is higher than
$`\delta_{h}`$, it indicates that the current sample is easy for the
reviser $`\hat{\mathcal{R}}`$ to revise, allowing for a major revision
of $`Y_{i}^{l}`$. If the reward $`s_{i}`$ is between $`\delta_{l}`$ and
$`\delta_{h}`$, it suggests that the reviser can improve the original
response moderately, so a minor revision is appropriate. Otherwise, if
the reward $`s_{i}`$ is lower than $`\delta_{l}`$, it indicates that the
reviser struggles to improve the response, and the original response
should remain unchanged. Moreover, considering that the reviser’s
ability may improve with further training on $`\mathcal{D}_{2}^{r}`$, we
further use a probability $`p`$ to control the proportion of revision
labels for samples in the latter two categories.

After assigning each prompt a revision label, we use the updated
$`\mathcal{D}_{2}^{r}`$ to continually train the preliminary reviser
using the NLL objective:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{L}=-\mathbb{E}_{(X_{i}^{r},Y_{i}^{l},r_{i},Y_{i}^{h})\sim\mathcal{D}_{2}^{r}}\left[\mathrm{log}P_{\theta}(r_{i},Y_{i}^{h}|X_{i}^{r},Y_{i}^{l})\right].
``` |  | (2) |

Unlike warm-up training, the adaptive reviser learns to predict the
revised response as well as the revision label based on the difficulty
of revising the initial response, thus implementing adaptive revisions.
The overall training process of the adaptive reviser is illustrated in
Algorithm

### 3.3 Alignment

In this section, we elaborate on the details of the proposed SEFT for
policy optimization. The SEFT process can be generally defined as
follows:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{P}=\mathbb{SEFT}(X^{p},\mathcal{P},\mathcal{G},\mathcal{R}),
``` |  | (3) |

where $`X^{p}=\{X_{j}^{p}\}|_{j=1}^{M}`$ denotes the set of unannotated
prompts for the optimization of policy $`\mathcal{P}`$, $`\mathcal{G}`$
is the generator used to generate an initial response
$`\hat{Y}_{j}^{p}`$ for each prompt $`X_{j}^{p}`$, and $`\mathcal{R}`$
is the adaptive reviser used to revise the initial response to obtain an
enhanced response $`Y_{j}^{p}`$. The prompts $`X^{p}`$ and the enhanced
responses $`Y^{p}=\{Y_{j}^{p}\}|_{j=1}^{M}`$ will be used to fine-tune
the policy $`\mathcal{P}`$ as follows:

|  |  |  |  |
|----|----|----|----|
|  |
``` math
\mathcal{P}=\arg\min_{\phi}-\mathbb{E}_{(X_{j}^{p},Y_{j}^{p})}\left[\mathrm{log}P_{\phi}(Y_{j}^{p}|X_{j}^{p})\right],
``` |  | (4) |

where $`\phi`$ refers to the parameters of the policy, typically
initialized from an SFT model \[[25](#bib.bib25)\].

The policy optimization with SEFT involves both internal and external
evolution:

#### Internal Evolution

In the internal evolution, the policy $`\mathcal{P}`$ is optimized using
its own generated responses, which are revised by the adaptive reviser
$`\mathcal{R}`$. This phase focuses on improving the policy within its
own response space. Acting as the generator $`\mathcal{G}`$ in Equation
[3](#S3.E3 "In 3.3 Alignment ‣ 3 SEFT: Self-Evolution Fine-Tuning ‣ Self-Evolution Fine-Tuning for Policy Optimization"),
the policy $`\mathcal{P}`$ first generates initial responses for the
prompts $`X^{p}`$. These initial responses are then refined by the
reviser $`\mathcal{R}`$ to produce a set of high-quality responses.
Finally, the policy $`\mathcal{P}`$ is fine-tuned using the prompts and
the revised responses as outlined in Equation
[4](#S3.E4 "In 3.3 Alignment ‣ 3 SEFT: Self-Evolution Fine-Tuning ‣ Self-Evolution Fine-Tuning for Policy Optimization"),
resulting in an updated policy $`\mathcal{P}_{I}`$.

#### External Evolution

In the external evolution phase, the policy $`\mathcal{P}_{I}`$ is
further fine-tuned using revised responses from an external robust
generator, which is the base model $`\mathcal{M}`$ of the reviser in
this study. This phase aims to enhance the policy’s capabilities by
exposing it to a higher-quality and more challenging response space,
thus facilitating more substantial improvements. Similar to the internal
evolution phase, the prompts $`X^{p}`$ and the revised responses
generated by the adaptive reviser from the initial responses of the
external generator $`\mathcal{M}`$ will be used to further fine-tune
$`\mathcal{P}_{I}`$ according to Equation
[4](#S3.E4 "In 3.3 Alignment ‣ 3 SEFT: Self-Evolution Fine-Tuning ‣ Self-Evolution Fine-Tuning for Policy Optimization"),
resulting in the final policy $`\mathcal{P}_{E}`$.

Note that while the external evolution phase ensures that the policy
benefits from superior examples, the integration of both phases enables
the policy model to progressively enhance its performance.

## 4 Experiments

We conduct extensive experiments to evaluate the effectiveness of our
proposed SEFT, specifically examining the reviser’s ability to enhance
the quality of initial responses and its impact on policy optimization.
Furthermore, we conduct analyses to explore the benefits of integrating
additional unlabeled data and the effects of progressive policy
optimization via internal and external evolution.

### 4.1 Experimental Setup

#### Training Datasets

We employ Nectar \[[42](#bib.bib42)\] for training the reviser. Nectar
is a high-quality dataset comprising 183K diverse dialogues, each prompt
containing seven responses generated from various models and ranked by
GPT-4. To prevent data contamination, prompts appearing in subsequent
evaluation benchmarks were filtered out. Then, we set aside 1.8K samples
as the test set for reviser evaluation. The remaining data is divided in
a 3:7 ratio, with one partition used for training the preliminary
reviser and the other for the adaptive reviser described in Section
[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Reviser Training ‣ 3 SEFT: Self-Evolution Fine-Tuning ‣ Self-Evolution Fine-Tuning for Policy Optimization").
In the Nectar dataset, Rank 0 represents the highest-quality response,
whereas Rank 6 represents the lowest-quality response. Our initial
responses are constructed by randomly selecting from responses ranked 1
to 6, with responses ranked 0 representing the final high-quality
responses. This ensures the training data covers a diverse range of
initial responses, enabling the reviser to acquire versatile
capabilities.

For policy optimization, we utilize UltraFeedback²² 2
[https://huggingface.co/datasets/openbmb/UltraFeedback](https://huggingface.co/datasets/openbmb/UltraFeedback)
\[[9](#bib.bib9)\], which comprises 64K prompts collected from diverse
sources. To validate that integrating more unannotated data consistently
enhances the performance of SEFT, we additionally sample subsets of 30K
and 60K prompts respectively from OpenHermes-2.5³³ 3
[https://huggingface.co/datasets/teknium/OpenHermes-2.5](https://huggingface.co/datasets/teknium/OpenHermes-2.5)
\[[29](#bib.bib29)\] as supplementary training data.

#### Training Details

During training the reviser model, we first explore implementing it with
OpenChat-3.5-7B \[[33](#bib.bib33)\]. Then, we compare the performance
with different base models, including OpenChat-3.5-7B
\[[33](#bib.bib33)\], Qwen1.5-32B-Chat \[[3](#bib.bib3)\], and
Yi-34B-Chat \[[37](#bib.bib37)\]. Striving for a balance between
efficiency and performance, we ultimately opt for Qwen1.5-32B-Chat to
implement the reviser. The reviser is trained on eight NVIDIA A800 GPUs
using DeepSpeed ZeRO 3. The batch size is set to 128, and the learning
rate scheduler follows the cosine type, with a warmup ratio of 0.03 and
a learning rate of 2e-5. The model undergoes training for 3 epochs, with
a maximum length of 2,048 tokens. To obtain the revision labels, we
utilize Starling-RM-7B-alpha \[[42](#bib.bib42)\] as the critic model
$`\mathcal{C}`$. The hyperparameters $`\delta_{l}`$, $`\delta_{h}`$, and
$`p`$ are set to 0.3, 1.0, and 0.8, respectively. The adaptive reviser
is initialized using the preliminary reviser with identical
hyperparameter settings and then trained continuously.

For the training of the policy model, we opt for Zephyr-7B-SFT-full
\[[32](#bib.bib32)\] as the backbone. Zephyr-7B-SFT-full and
Qwen1.5-32B-Chat are respectively employed as the generators for the
internal evolution and external evolution phases, as mentioned in
Section
[3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Alignment ‣ 3 SEFT: Self-Evolution Fine-Tuning ‣ Self-Evolution Fine-Tuning for Policy Optimization").
Similar to the reviser, training is conducted using DeepSpeed ZeRO 3 on
eight NVIDIA A800 GPUs. The total batch size is set to 128, with a
cosine learning rate scheduler, a warmup ratio of 0.1, and a learning
rate of 5e-7. The model is trained for 3 epochs for each evolution, with
a maximum length of 2,048 tokens.

#### Evaluation

To assess the reviser’s effectiveness in enhancing response quality, we
adopt methodologies from prior research \[[19](#bib.bib19),
[16](#bib.bib16)\] and utilize established reward models to evaluate
both the initial and revised responses across the Nectar test set.
Specifically, we employ four robust reward models:
FsfairX-LLaMA3-RM-v0.1 \[[11](#bib.bib11)\], Eurus-RM-7B
\[[38](#bib.bib38)\], Starling-RM-7B-alpha \[[42](#bib.bib42)\], and
RM-Mistral-7B \[[35](#bib.bib35)\]. The assessments generated by these
reward models are then aggregated to determine the reviser’s performance
and overall success rate.

For the policy evaluation, we employ AlpacaEval 2.0 \[[12](#bib.bib12)\]
and MT-Bench \[[41](#bib.bib41)\]. AlpacaEval 2.0 includes 805
instructions and assesses the model’s success rate against GPT-4 for
each instruction, using an evaluator based on GPT-4. MT-Bench
\[[41](#bib.bib41)\] consists of 80 multi-turn dialogues spanning eight
domains, with GPT-4 rating the model’s responses on a scale from 1 to
10.

#### Baselines

We first compare the performance of our adaptive reviser with the
following methods: (i) Aligner \[[15](#bib.bib15)\] primarily aims to
enhance the usefulness and safety of a primary LLM by training a smaller
LLM to refine the outputs. For a fair comparison, we implement Aligner
on the same training set as our reviser. (ii) Label reviser is similar
to our adaptive reviser but assigns revision labels based on the
disparity in rank between the initial response and the target response
in the Nectar training set, whereas our revision labels are determined
by the performance of the preliminary reviser. For more details, refer
to Appendix
[B](#A2 "Appendix B Implementation Details ‣ Self-Evolution Fine-Tuning for Policy Optimization").
(iii) Preliminary reviser, as described in Section
[3.2](#S3.SS2 "3.2 Reviser Training ‣ 3 SEFT: Self-Evolution Fine-Tuning ‣ Self-Evolution Fine-Tuning for Policy Optimization"),
is trained using only 30% of the training data and tries to revise all
low-quality initial responses. (iv) Original response denotes the
original input responses to a reviser, which serve as a naive baseline.

Next, we compare the proposed SEFT with other policy optimization
methods: (i) SFT directly optimizes the policy model using prompts and
*chosen* responses from the UltraFeedback dataset. (ii) DPO
\[[25](#bib.bib25)\] optimizes the policy model by applying a reward
modeling objective to preference data. (iii) ORPO \[[14](#bib.bib14)\]
presents a reference model-free method that integrates the odds ratio of
the *chosen* response over the *rejected* response into the SFT
objective function. This integration serves to penalize the probability
of generating the rejected response directly.

## 5 Conclusion

In this paper, we introduce self-evolution fine-tuning (SEFT) for
alignment of large language models (LLMs). SEFT employs an adaptive
reviser to enhance the overall quality of initial responses by making
revisions only when improvements are feasible. Our experiments show that
the adaptive reviser consistently enhances response quality and
outperforms baseline methods. The effectiveness of SEFT in policy
optimization is validated through extensive experiments on benchmarks
such as AlpacaEval 2.0 and MT-Bench. Notably, we highlight SEFT’s
capability to leverage unannotated data to improve response quality
while maintaining stability and efficiency in policy optimization.

We trained the adaptive revisers of three different sizes based on
various backbones and tested their performance on our Nectar test set.
The benefit score is defined as:
$`s_{i}=\texttt{Score}(\hat{Y}_{i}^{l})-\texttt{Score}(Y_{i}^{l})`$,
where $`\texttt{Score}(Y_{i}^{l})`$ and
$`\texttt{Score}(\hat{Y}_{i}^{l})`$ are the reward scores of the
original response and revised response evaluated by reward model,
respectively. We utilized the four reward models mentioned above to
obtain the benefit scores. Additionally, we analyzed the distribution of
revision labels when revisers corrected the original responses.

As shown in Table
[3](#A1.T3 "Table 3 ‣ Appendix A Performance of Adaptive Reviser ‣ Self-Evolution Fine-Tuning for Policy Optimization"),
all revisers exhibit consistently superior performance when handling
responses of varying quality. Specifically, when the original response
quality is low, the reviser’s improvement yields a significantly high
benefit. For instance, the reviser based on Qwen1.5-32B-Chat achieves
benefit scores of +4.01, +383.56, +3.76, and +3.62 across the four RMs
when revising Rank 6 responses. Conversely, when the initial response
quality is high (GPT-4 level), the reviser still manages to bring about
stable quality enhancements. Furthermore, we observed that even the 7B
reviser (OpenChat-3.5-7B) could improve high-quality original responses,
despite the original responses being generated by models larger than 7B.
The distribution of revision labels indicates that as the original
response quality increases, the proportion of \[No Revise\] labels
output by the reviser also increases. This aligns with our primary goal
for implementing revision labels: to ensure that revisions are made only
when necessary, preserving high-quality responses as they are. On the
other hand, as the quality of the initial response decreases, the
proportion of \[Major Revise\] labels increases, signaling a more
extensive revision effort by the reviser. The \[Minor Revise\] label
appears more frequently for responses of moderate quality. This is
expected because the \[Minor Revise\] label acts as a middle ground
between \[No Revise\] and \[Major Revise\].

The experimental results in Table
[4](#A1.T4 "Table 4 ‣ Appendix A Performance of Adaptive Reviser ‣ Self-Evolution Fine-Tuning for Policy Optimization")
highlight the exceptional performance of the adaptive reviser compared
to baseline methods. Across all reward models (RM1-RM4), the adaptive
reviser achieves the highest average benefit scores, indicating its
overall superiority. Notably, the adaptive reviser shows significant
improvements over the original responses, particularly for lower-quality
inputs, with greater benefit scores as the initial response quality
declines. Furthermore, for high-quality original responses, the adaptive
reviser consistently outperforms other revisers. For instance, under
RM2, the adaptive reviser achieves benefit scores of +9.64 and +19.58
for Rank 0 and 1, respectively, while other methods show considerably
lower or even negative scores. These results demonstrate that the
adaptive reviser excels in refining both high and low-quality responses.

## Appendix B Implementation Details

The hyperparameters for our implementations are listed in Table

For the label reviser, we utilize three revision labels: \[No Revise\],
\[Major Revise\], and \[Minor Revise\]. Specifically, 10% of the Nectar
training samples with original responses ranked at 0 are labeled as \[No
Revise\]. For the remaining samples, those with original response
rankings between 1 and 3 receive the \[Minor Revise\] label, while all
other samples are assigned the \[Major Revise\] label.
````
